<Callout tone="primary" title="Updated October 6, 2026">
This post first covered the suspension in September 2025. The rules have moved since, so we've added a section on where de minimis stands today. The short version: it's still gone.
</Callout>

## What changed on August 29, 2025

For years, a shipment valued at $800 or less could enter the United States free of duty. This was the "de minimis" exemption under 19 U.S.C. 1321, and it's a big part of how cross-border ecommerce got so cheap.

Executive Order 14324 ended that for every country starting August 29, 2025. From that date, low-value shipments had to pay duty like any other import.

## What low-value shipments paid at first

When the suspension started, the main tariffs on most imports were the IEEPA tariffs (the "reciprocal" and fentanyl-related duties). For shipments arriving by mail, carriers could pay one of two ways for the first six months:

1. **The ad valorem IEEPA rate** for the country of origin, charged on the value of the item.
2. **A flat amount per item**, based on that country's IEEPA rate:

| Country's IEEPA rate | Duty per item |
|---|---|
| Less than 16% | $80 |
| 16% to 25% | $160 |
| More than 25% | $200 |

Shipments arriving any other way (express couriers, freight) had to go through normal formal or informal entry and pay every duty that applied.

## Where de minimis stands now

On February 20, 2026, the Supreme Court ruled in *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump* that IEEPA doesn't give the President the power to impose tariffs. The IEEPA tariffs stopped being collected, and their HTS headings went inactive on February 24, 2026.

A lot of people assumed de minimis would come back with that ruling. It didn't:

- **February 20, 2026:** a new executive order, "Continuing the Suspension of Duty-Free De Minimis Treatment for All Countries," kept the suspension in place on a new legal footing.
- **June 24, 2026:** CBP published two interim final rules making the suspension indefinite, one for mail and one for every other mode of transport.
- **August 13, 2026:** the Court of International Trade granted summary judgment for the government, upholding the suspension.

So in practice, a $40 order pays duty the same way a $40,000 container does. The rates that apply are the HTS base rate plus whatever Chapter 99 tariffs cover that product from that country, such as Section 232 on steel and aluminum content, or Section 301 on goods from China.

<Callout tone="warning" title="The flat per-item fees are gone">
The $80, $160 and $200 per-item amounts were tied to IEEPA rates. With the IEEPA tariffs gone, mail shipments pay the duties that actually apply to the product.
</Callout>

## What this means for importers

If you sell into the US through low-value parcels, every SKU now needs a correct HTS code and a known duty rate. That sounds like paperwork, but it's really a pricing problem: a product that paid nothing in 2024 can now carry a base rate plus one or more Chapter 99 tariffs, and those change with almost every HTS revision.

The fastest way to see what a specific product pays today is to run its HTS code and country of origin through a duty calculator that stacks every program, instead of guessing from a headline rate.
