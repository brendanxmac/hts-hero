## What a tariff is

A tariff is a tax on goods coming into a country. When something is imported into the United States, US Customs and Border Protection (CBP) collects a duty on it before it can be released into the country.

That duty is almost always a percentage of the goods' customs value. A $10,000 shipment with a 5% tariff owes $500. Some products pay a fixed amount per unit instead, like 3.4 cents per kilogram, and a few pay both.

## Who actually pays it

The **importer of record** pays. That's the company or person responsible for the goods when they enter the US, usually the buyer, sometimes a customs broker or a seller shipping on the buyer's behalf.

The foreign seller doesn't send money to the US government. In practice the cost spreads out: importers raise prices, suppliers sometimes cut theirs, and customers often end up paying some of it. But the legal obligation, and the risk of getting it wrong, sits with the importer.

## How the US decides what you owe

Every product that enters the US is classified under a code in the **Harmonized Tariff Schedule of the United States (HTSUS)**, published by the US International Trade Commission. The code is 10 digits long. The first six are shared worldwide (the Harmonized System); the last four are specific to the US.

For a given product, three things set the duty:

1. **The HTS code.** It decides the base rate. A cotton T-shirt (`6109.10.00`) pays 16.5%; most medicines pay nothing.
2. **The country of origin.** Where the product was made, which isn't always where it shipped from. Origin decides which column of rates applies and which additional tariffs kick in.
3. **The entry date.** Rates change. The duty is set by the rules in force on the day the goods enter.

The value matters too, since most rates are a percentage of it.

## Base rates: the three columns

Each line of the HTS has three rate columns:

| Column | Who it applies to |
|---|---|
| **General** | Most countries: anyone with normal trade relations with the US |
| **Special** | Goods that qualify for a free trade agreement or preference program, such as USMCA, when the importer claims it |
| **Column 2** | Countries without normal trade relations: currently Cuba, North Korea, Russia and Belarus |

The General rate is what most people mean by "the tariff" on a product. For many goods it's modest, often under 5%.

## Additional tariffs: Chapter 99

On top of the base rate, the US adds tariffs through **Chapter 99** of the HTS. Each one has its own heading, like `9903.82.02`, with legal notes that say which products and countries it covers. These are where the big numbers come from.

As of October 2026, the main ones in force are:

- **Section 232 (national security):** steel, aluminum and copper (up to 50%), autos and auto parts (25%), heavy trucks, semiconductors, wood products, patented pharmaceuticals and drones.
- **Section 301 China:** 7.5% to 100% on most Chinese goods, depending on the product.
- **Section 301 forced labor:** 10% or 12.5% on goods from about 55 countries, with lower all-in rates for the EU, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan and Switzerland, from July 24, 2026.
- **Section 301 Brazil:** 25% on Brazilian goods, from July 22, 2026.
- **Section 338 Canada:** 50% on a list of Canadian products, from August 22, 2026.
- **Section 201 quartz:** a safeguard on quartz countertops and slabs.

Several of these come with exemptions, and some replace others instead of adding to them. Our guide to [US tariff stacking rules](/blog/us-tariff-stacking-rules) walks through how they combine.

<Callout tone="primary" title="What happened to the reciprocal tariffs?">
The "reciprocal" and fentanyl tariffs of 2025 were imposed under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA). On February 20, 2026, the Supreme Court ruled that IEEPA doesn't authorize tariffs, and those duties stopped from February 24. A temporary 10% Section 122 surcharge replaced them until July 23, 2026, and the Section 301 forced-labor duties started the next day.
</Callout>

## Fees on top of duty

Two customs fees apply to most commercial imports:

- **Merchandise Processing Fee (MPF):** 0.3464% of the value on formal entries, with a minimum of $34.58 and a maximum of $670.86 for fiscal year 2027, which started October 1, 2026.
- **Harbor Maintenance Fee (HMF):** 0.125% of the value, on ocean shipments only.

## Why tariffs are hard to get right

The math itself is simple. Knowing which rules apply is the hard part:

- **The schedule changes all the time.** The HTS was revised 20 times in 2026 through the end of September, and most revisions changed at least one Chapter 99 tariff.
- **Exemptions depend on facts about your goods.** Whether a product contains steel, whether a drug is generic, whether goods qualify for USMCA: each can move the duty by tens of percentage points.
- **Programs interact.** Some tariffs stack. Others switch off when a Section 232 tariff applies. Getting the order wrong gives you the wrong total.

That's why an HTS code and a headline rate aren't enough to price an import. You need the full stack, for your specific code, origin and date. Our guide to [how US tariffs are calculated](/blog/how-us-tariffs-are-calculated) shows the whole process with real numbers.
