## The default: everything adds up

When more than one tariff covers a product, the starting rule is simple: they **stack**. The base rate, plus each Chapter 99 tariff that applies, all charged on the same customs value.

The exceptions are written into the HTS itself, in two forms:

- **Exemption headings** that switch a tariff off in specific cases, like `9903.05.90`, which removes the forced-labor duty from Section 232 goods.
- **Non-stacking notes** in the U.S. notes to Chapter 99, which say that one program doesn't apply to goods covered by another.

Below is how the programs in force in October 2026 combine.

## Section 301 China stacks on everything

The Section 301 tariffs on Chinese goods (Lists 1 to 3 at 25%, List 4A at 7.5%, and the 2024 to 2026 increases of 25% to 100% on products like semiconductors, solar cells, EVs and medical gloves) apply on top of every other tariff.

That includes Section 232. A steel article from China in October 2026 pays the base rate, 50% under Section 232 and 25% under Section 301 List 3: **77.9% in total** on HTS `7326.90.86.88`.

## Country programs give way to Section 232

Three newer country-wide tariffs switch off when a Section 232 tariff applies to the product:

| Tariff | Rate | Exemption for Section 232 goods |
|---|---|---|
| Section 301 forced labor | 10% or 12.5% by country | `9903.05.90` |
| Section 301 Brazil | 25% | `9903.05.07` |
| Section 338 Canada, dairy and food list (b)(2) | 50% | `9903.03.15` |

"Section 232 goods" here means goods that actually pay one of the Section 232 duty headings: metals, autos and parts, heavy trucks, semiconductors, wood products or patented pharmaceuticals. Two things don't count:

- **Exemptions from Section 232.** A product that claims "contains no steel, aluminum or copper" (`9903.82.01`) isn't paying Section 232, so the forced-labor duty applies to it.
- **The drone tariffs.** Section 232 duties on unmanned aircraft stack with the country programs instead of replacing them.

<Callout tone="warning" title="Canada changed on September 15, 2026">
When the Section 338 Canada tariff started on August 22, Section 232 goods were exempt from all of it. Since September 15, the exemption only covers the dairy and food list. Section 232 goods on the other two Canada lists now pay the 50% on top. On $10,000 of Canadian steel furniture, that took the duty from $2,500 to $7,500.

Since September 29, certain Canadian alcoholic beverages, dairy products and motor vehicle products are banned from import altogether (Proclamations 11061 to 11063), so no stacking question arises for them.
</Callout>

## Country programs that stack with each other

- **Brazil:** the 25% Section 301 Brazil tariff and the 12.5% forced-labor duty both apply, for 37.5% on top of the base rate on a non-exempt product.
- **China:** the Section 301 China list rate and the 12.5% forced-labor duty both apply, unless a Section 232 tariff applies.
- **Canada:** the 50% Section 338 tariff applies even with a USMCA claim. The 10% forced-labor duty for Canada drops off when the goods are claimed under USMCA.

## Inside Section 232: one program per product

Section 232 now covers seven product groups (metals, autos, heavy trucks, semiconductors, wood, pharmaceuticals and drones), and when a product could fall under more than one, the HTS notes pick a winner:

| When a product is covered by... | Which applies | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| Autos, heavy trucks or semiconductors **and** metals | Autos, heavy trucks or semiconductors | U.S. notes 33, 38 and 39 |
| Semiconductors **and** autos or heavy trucks | Semiconductors | U.S. note 39(a) |
| Auto or heavy truck parts **and** wood | Auto or heavy truck parts | U.S. notes 33 and 38(h) |
| Taiwan civil aircraft components **and** metals | Aircraft exemption | U.S. note 35(c) |

Within each program, the headings are mutually exclusive too: a metals product pays one of the `9903.82` headings, a pharmaceutical one of `9903.04.60` to `.70`, a drone one of `9903.08.21` to `.26`.

One catch: the auto and heavy truck parts tariffs often depend on a fact the importer has to confirm (that the part is for a vehicle). Until that's confirmed, an auto part made of steel still pays the metals tariff.

## All-in rates: how the deals work

The 2025 deals with the EU, Japan and South Korea were originally written into IEEPA headings, which ended on February 24, 2026. What survives lives inside the product and country programs, as **all-in rates**: the tariff tops the total duty up to a set level.

| Program | EU | Japan | South Korea | Taiwan | Switzerland | UK |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 232 autos and parts | 15% all-in | 15% all-in | 15% all-in | Parts 15% all-in | | Cars +7.5%, parts +10% |
| 232 wood furniture and cabinets | 15% all-in | 15% all-in | 15% all-in | 15% all-in | | +10% |
| 232 patented pharmaceuticals | 15% all-in | 15% all-in | 15% all-in | | 15% all-in | 0% |
| 232 drones | 15% all-in | 15% all-in | 15% all-in | 15% all-in | 15% all-in | +10% |
| 301 forced labor | 10% all-in | 12.5% all-in | 12.5% all-in | 10% all-in | 12.5% all-in | +10% |

"All-in" means the tariff adds whatever brings base rate plus tariff to the level shown. On a product with a 4% base rate, a 15% all-in tariff adds 11%. If the base rate is already 15% or higher, it adds nothing.

## A quick way to check your own stack

For any product, walk the list in this order:

1. Does a **Section 232** duty heading apply? If yes, the forced-labor, Brazil and Canada dairy-and-food tariffs are off.
2. If the goods are **Chinese**, add the Section 301 China rate for the product's list.
3. If no Section 232 duty applies, add the **forced-labor** rate for the country, the **Brazil** 25% for Brazilian goods, and any **Canada 338** list the product is on.
4. For Canada lists (b)(1) and (b)(3), add the 50% **even if** Section 232 applies.
5. Apply **all-in** rates last, against the base rate.

It's a lot to hold in your head for one product, and impossible across a catalog. A calculator that shows each heading it applied, and each one it switched off with the reason, is the only practical way to be sure.
