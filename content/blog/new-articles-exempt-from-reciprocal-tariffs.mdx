<Callout tone="primary" title="Updated October 6, 2026">
The reciprocal tariff ended after the Supreme Court's IEEPA ruling on February 20, 2026. We've kept this list because it still matters for reconciling entries made before then, and because the lesson about partial exemptions applies to plenty of tariffs still in force.
</Callout>

In November 2025 the US exempted **237 more HTS subheadings** of food and agricultural products from the IEEPA reciprocal tariff, plus **11 partial exemptions** that covered only one named product inside a subheading. The exemptions applied to goods entered on or after November 13, 2025, and lasted until the reciprocal tariff itself ended in February 2026.

## What changed in November 2025

Executive Order 14360, "Modifying the Scope of the Reciprocal Tariff With Respect to Certain Agricultural Products," signed on November 14, 2025, added **237 subheadings** to the list of goods exempt from the reciprocal tariff. It applied retroactively to goods entered on or after **12:01 a.m. EST on November 13, 2025**.

Most were food and farm products the US imports in volume or doesn't grow much of: coffee and tea, bananas and other tropical fruits, oranges, tomatoes, beef, juices, cocoa, spices, and some fertilizers. CBP's guidance (CSMS #66814923) told importers to claim them in two ways:

| Heading | What it covered |
|---|---|
| `9903.01.32` | The 237 subheadings added to the Annex II exemption list, exempt in full |
| `9903.02.78` | 11 subheadings where only a named product was exempt |

The interesting part was that second group. For these **11 partial exemptions**, the exemption covered one specific product inside an HTS subheading, and every other product in that subheading still paid.

| `0805.90.01` | Etrogs |
| `0811.90.80` | Tropical fruit, nesoi, frozen, whether or not previously steamed or boiled |

| `1905.90.10` | Bread, pastry, cakes, biscuits and similar baked products, nesoi, and puddings, for religious purposes only |
| `1905.90.90` | Bakers' wares, communion wafers, sealing wafers, rice paper and similar products, nesoi, for religious purposes only |

| `3301.29.51` | Essential oils other than those of citrus fruit, nesoi, for religious purposes only |

## Why partial exemptions matter

A 10-digit HTS code usually decides the duty. Partial exemptions break that rule: two products under the same code can pay different amounts depending on what they actually are.

That pattern didn't go away with the reciprocal tariff. Section 232 and Section 301 both have exemptions written around product descriptions and end uses, and they're easy to miss if you only look at the code. The [Section 301 China exclusions](/blog/china-tariff-exemption-extensions) are a live example: many are narrower than the subheading they sit under. A classification that records *why* a product fits its code, with the description to back it up, is what lets you claim them with confidence.

## When the reciprocal tariff ended

On February 20, 2026, the Supreme Court held in *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump* that IEEPA doesn't authorize tariffs. CBP stopped collecting the reciprocal tariff, and the other IEEPA tariffs, from **February 24, 2026**. The tariffs that replaced it are covered in [Latest US Tariff Changes](/blog/latest-us-tariff-changes).

If you entered exempt products between November 13, 2025 and February 23, 2026 and paid the reciprocal tariff on them anyway, or paid reciprocal duty on any entry before it ended, those were IEEPA duties. CBP handles IEEPA refunds through CAPE declarations in the ACE Portal (see [CBP's IEEPA duty refund page](https://www.cbp.gov/trade/programs-administration/trade-remedies/ieepa-duty-refunds)), so check with your customs broker on the status of your entries.
