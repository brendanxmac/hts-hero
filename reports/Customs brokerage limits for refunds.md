# Protests open, every other refund door stays shut

Without a broker license, HTS Hero can legally prepare, sign and file only one refund instrument for a paying importer: a **protest under 19 CFR 174.3, backed by a power of attorney**. CBP has held this in four published rulings from 1998 to 2017 (HQ 114199, 114404, 115248, H272798). Drawback claims, post-summary corrections (PSCs) and CAPE IEEPA refund declarations are all "customs business" under 19 U.S.C. 1641(a)(2). That remains true whether HTS Hero files them, the importer files them, or a broker files them, because the statute covers preparation "whether or not signed or filed by the preparer." CAPE is narrower still: CBP lets only the importer of record or the broker who filed the entries submit a declaration, and attorneys are excluded. Section 122 has no CBP refund mechanism at all. On money, a broker may not pay HTS Hero a commission, a percentage, or a per-importer or per-entry fee (19 CFR 111.36(b), HQ H276784, HQ H350722). CBP has said, as dicta, that a flat fee "not tied to a particular transaction" would be permissible. On features, the entry audit, the rate lookups and broker-facing reports can sit on the permitted side if they *identify* discrepancies and leave every filing decision to the importer or its broker. The Managed promise to keep imports "in the lowest-duty position" goes further: it describes deciding classification and program claims for future entries, which CBP treats as licensed work. Everything in this report is CBP's administrative position plus my inference from it. No court has tested the protest exception, and each ruling binds only its own facts, so a customs attorney should sign off before HTS Hero sells any filing service.

## The statute makes refund paperwork broker work, no matter who signs it

The starting point is the definition. 19 U.S.C. 1641(a)(2) defines customs business as transactions with CBP concerning classification, valuation, "the payment of duties ... or the refund, rebate, or drawback thereof," and adds that it "also includes the preparation of documents or forms in any format ... intended to be filed with U.S. Customs and Border Protection in furtherance of such activities, **whether or not signed or filed by the preparer**, or activities relating to such preparation, but does not include the mere electronic transmission of data received for transmission to Customs" ([19 U.S.C. 1641](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/19/1641)). Section 1641(b)(1) then says: "No person may conduct customs business (other than solely on behalf of that person) unless that person holds a valid customs broker's license." The penalty for intentional violation is up to **$10,000 per transaction** (1641(b)(6)), and 19 CFR 111.91(b) caps a non-broker's total at **$30,000** ([19 CFR 111.91](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/19/111.91)). None of the notes verified whether those figures have been adjusted for inflation.

The regulation in [19 CFR 111.1](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-111/subpart-A/section-111.1) tracks the statute and carves out only two things: mere electronic transmission and "corporate compliance activity." Corporate compliance activity is limited to entities related by more than 50% ownership and "does not extend to the actual preparation or filing of the documents." The license exceptions in [19 CFR 111.2(a)(2)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-111/subpart-A/section-111.2) cover an importer "transacting customs business solely on his own account," that importer's "authorized regular employees or officers," a broker's own employees, and marine, in-bond, noncommercial and foreign-trade-zone cases. There is **no exception for consultants, software vendors, refund firms, drawback specialists or attorneys**. One earlier note suggested that software preparing data for the importer might fit the "mere transmission" or "corporate compliance" carve-outs. The text rules that out: HTS Hero is not related to its customers, and software that selects data is not a conduit for data "received for transmission."

CBP has rejected the "the client signs it" defense directly. HQ 114199 (Feb. 26, 1998) told an unlicensed drawback consultant: "you would be conducting customs business without a license if you prepare, or help clients to prepare, forms or documents that will be filed with Customs. **The fact that the client will sign and file them is immaterial**" ([HQ 114199](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/114199)). Handing the work to a broker does not help either. HQ H326926 (Dec. 19, 2023) held that "Identifying entry-related data and keying it into an ABI system falls squarely within the scope of preparing parts of an entry intended to be filed with CBP," even though the broker would "audit the data, make necessary amendments, and transmit" ([HQ H326926](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/H326926)). HQ H350722 (Jan. 16, 2026) extended this to automation: "whether data is extracted manually or automatically though an OCR tool, CBP has repeatedly held that an unlicensed entity cannot decide what data should appear on an entry," and "the filing of an entry is not a prerequisite to such an activity constituting customs business" ([HQ H350722](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/H350722)).

Each refund channel then adds its own filer rules. For **drawback**, [19 CFR 190.6](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/19/190.6) lists who may sign: corporate officers or employees "legally authorized to bind the corporation," a full partner, a sole proprietor, "Any employee of the business entity with a power of attorney," an individual acting for himself, or a licensed broker with a power of attorney. The list has no outside-agent category. Claims must go "through a CBP-authorized system" ([19 CFR 190.51](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/19/190.51)). In HQ 113715 (Jan. 9, 1997), a consultant whose service "results in the discovery of a drawback opportunity" and who then paid a broker to file was held to put the broker in violation ([HQ 113715](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/113715)). For **PSCs**, CBP says "The trade is responsible for submitting a PSC" through ACE, within 300 days of entry and at least 15 days before scheduled liquidation ([CBP PSC page](https://cbp.gov/trade/programs-administration/entry-summary/post-summary-correction)). An older ICPA guide gives 270 days; CBP's current page controls. H272798 states plainly: "Post-Entry Amendments and Post-Summary Corrections are considered 'customs business' and must be performed by a licensed customs broker" ([HQ H272798](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/H272798)). The importer can still file its own PSC if it has ABI filer status. For **CAPE**, CBP's IEEPA refund page states: "Only the IOR or the licensed customs broker who filed the entries can file a CAPE Declaration." CBP answers "No" to whether an attorney may file for an importer, and refunds go only "to the Importer of Record or the Notify Party" ([CBP IEEPA Duty Refunds](https://www.cbp.gov/trade/programs-administration/trade-remedies/ieepa-duty-refunds)). For **Section 122**, CBP has no refund portal. The CIT struck the 10% surcharge down 2-1 in May 2026, relief was limited to the plaintiffs, and the Federal Circuit stayed the judgment ([Skadden](https://www.skadden.com/insights/publications/2026/05/us-trade-court-strikes-down-section-122-tariffs)). Practitioners recommend protective protests, but at least one firm argues the duties may not be protestable at all ([Tariff Refund Solutions](https://tariffrefundsolutions.com/section-122-tariff-refund)).

## Protests are the one exception, and the full-text rulings settle the conflict

The earlier research notes disagreed on protests, and the disagreement matters. The first protest note concluded that preparing a protest for an importer is "Not allowed without a broker license." It reasoned from 1641(a)(2)'s "whether or not signed or filed by the preparer" language and read 174.3's "other agent" category as limited to related or non-commercial agents. The licensing note likewise advised that "The importer should sign its own protest." The drawback note cited HQ 114199 from a mirror site and flagged the protest carve-out without checking 174.3. The final note pulled the rulings in full from CBP's CROSS database and found that **CBP has said the opposite, consistently, for nearly three decades**. The full-text reading wins. The first note's reasoning is a reasonable reading of the statute, but CBP has expressly declined to apply it to protests.

The regulatory hook is [19 CFR 174.3(a)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-174): "When a protest is filed by a person acting as agent or attorney in fact for the principal, other than an attorney at law or a customhouse broker or his authorized employee acting in his behalf, there shall have been filed or shall be filed with the protest a power of attorney which either specifically authorizes such agent to make, sign, and file the protest or grants unlimited authority to such agent." Section 174.12(a)(7) (the current numbering; H272798 and 115248 cite it as (a)(6)) lets protests be filed by "Any authorized agent of any of the persons described in paragraphs (a)(1) through (6) of this section, subject to the provisions of § 174.3." The statute behind it is 19 U.S.C. 1514(c)(2)(F): "any authorized agent of any of the persons described in clauses (A) through (E)" ([19 U.S.C. 1514](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/19/1514)).

CBP's four rulings read that text as a deliberate exception:

| Ruling | Facts | What CBP said |
|---|---|---|
| HQ 114199 (Feb. 26, 1998) | Unlicensed ex-Customs employee starting a paid drawback consultancy | "you may prepare, sign and file protests if you have obtained authority from an importer in the form of a properly executed power of attorney. This is an exception to the rule that prohibits the involvement of unlicensed consultants in actual transactions with Customs. The authority for this exception is found in 19 CFR §174.3." Holding: no preparing filed documents "unless the document is a protest and the consultant has been issued a power of attorney to file the protest." ([HQ 114199](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/114199)) |
| HQ 114404 (Mar. 16, 1999) | DuPont's unlicensed unit filing for separate companies | "The definition of 'agent' in these two circumstances is not limited to customs brokers but may be any agent duly empowered to represent the importer for such purposes." ([HQ 114404](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/114404)) |
| HQ 115248 (Aug. 28, 2001) | Casio's unlicensed affiliate that "bills the subsidiaries for its services" | "CCA, if duly authorized by the importer, may file ruling requests and protests on behalf of its sister corporations, because current regulations allow any authorized agent to do so." ([HQ 115248](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/115248)) |
| HQ H272798 (Jan. 26, 2017) | Unlicensed Canadian consultant paid by a multinational | "pursuant to 19 C.F.R. § 174.3, an unlicensed consultant can prepare, sign, and file a protest if a properly executed power of attorney has been obtained." The holding lists "preparing ruling requests and protests" among activities that are not unlawful customs business. ([HQ H272798](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/H272798)) |

Two details in the full texts answer the first note's objections. First, 114199 dealt with the same "whether or not signed or filed by the preparer" language and still carved out protests, so CBP saw the statutory argument and rejected it for this instrument. Second, the consultants in 114199 and H272798 were paid commercial services, and the 115248 affiliate billed for its work. A for-hire business is therefore within the facts CBP approved, not outside them. The 2022 broker modernization rule did not touch the exception. "Protest" appears once in its text, in an unrelated passage, and the rule endorsed H272798 as guidance "to other brokers who encounter the same scenarios" ([87 FR 63267](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2022-10-18/html/2022-22445.htm)). A CROSS search found no later ruling that limits or revokes the exception. The full-text pass also turned up three citation errors in the sources. H272798 cites "HQ 114119," which is plainly 114199. It and 115248 cite 174.12(a)(6), which is now (a)(7). The 2022 Federal Register preamble misdates H272798 as 2018 and 115248 as 2011.

The exception has firm edges, and HTS Hero should design to them. **HTS Hero may not represent the importer alone before CBP.** HQ 114404 says "it would be improper for an unlicensed consultant to appear alone before Customs to discuss customs business matters on their client's behalf." Follow-up calls with a CBP Center about a protest therefore need the importer present. **The analysis that leads to the protest is judged separately.** H272798 approved audits only "to the extent the reviews will not lead to the preparation of Customs documents." It did not say whether an audit that feeds a permitted protest is itself covered, so that question is unsettled. **A protest is not a PSC.** For unliquidated entries the refund route is a PSC, and that stays broker work. **Fees are unaddressed.** No ruling conditions the exception on how the agent is paid. That supports a flat or subscription fee, but a contingency fee charged directly to the importer rests on silence, not a holding. **Court cases end the exception.** CIT Rule 75(b)(1) requires every party other than an individual pro se to "appear through an attorney authorized to practice before the court" ([CIT Slip Op. 22-80](https://www.cit.uscourts.gov/sites/cit/files/22-80.pdf)). Any 1581(a) challenge to a denied protest, and any 1581(i) suit, needs a CIT-admitted lawyer.

The exception also matters less for IEEPA than it first appears. The government's position, as reported in September 2026, is that older IEEPA entries need a 1581(i) suit followed by a court-ordered reliquidation and CAPE. In that view, an IEEPA-only protest is neither clearly required nor clearly sufficient ([Ward and Smith](https://www.wardandsmith.com/article/after-learning-resources-liquidation-protests-and-ieepa-tariff-refunds)). CAPE Phase 3, reported to open Oct. 6, 2026 for CIT plaintiffs with reliquidation orders, is confirmed only by secondary sources ([Coppersmith](https://www.coppersmith.com/cape-phase-3-ieepa-refunds-october-2026/)). The protest exception is strongest for ordinary disputes over classification or rates, Section 301 exclusion refunds on liquidated entries ([ArentFox Schiff](https://www.afslaw.com/perspectives/alerts/customs-issues-guidance-how-preserve-your-rights-refunds-china-section-301)), and protective Section 122 protests, if those duties prove protestable. One practical risk cuts against using it casually: a protest that is late, vague or improperly authorized can forfeit CIT jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. 1581(a). An error here can cost the client the refund itself. Two further questions sit outside CBP's rulings entirely. One is whether drafting a protest's legal arguments for pay is unauthorized practice of law under state law. The other is whether ACE's protest module accepts a non-broker, non-importer agent; paper Form 19 under 174.12(b) is the fallback. Neither was answered by any source.

## Brokers cannot pay HTS Hero per client, per entry or per refund

The referral question turns on a rule that binds the broker, not HTS Hero. [19 CFR 111.36(b)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-111/subpart-C/section-111.36) says: "a broker must not enter into any agreement with an unlicensed person to transact customs business for others in such manner that the fees or other benefits resulting from the services rendered for others inure to the benefit of the unlicensed person." The only exception, 111.36(c), covers freight forwarders. Section 111.1 defines a forwarder as someone "engaged in the business of dispatching shipments in foreign commerce," which a tariff software company is not. Refund, PSC and drawback work are customs business by definition, so the ban covers refund revenue exactly as it covers entry fees.

CBP has applied the rule to every payment shape HTS Hero might propose. In HQ H276784 (Dec. 29, 2016), a broker wanted to pay two non-profits "a commission plan based fee that is calculated as a percentage of the entry fee," paid "per importer or per shipment." CBP held: "Unlicensed independent agents, may not accept commissions from Welke for promoting its brokerage services." It did not matter that the importer was billed directly and dealt with the broker directly ([HQ H276784](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/H276784)). In the 2022 final rule, CBP refused a sales-representative carve-out: "agreements wherein unlicensed persons acting as independent agents receive a commission for marketing or selling customs services on behalf of a brokerage company are generally prohibited." In the same passage it offered the one opening: "**a flat fee, not tied to a particular transaction, would be permissible** to compensate third-party agents for selling customs services" ([87 FR 63290](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2022/10/18/2022-22445/modernization-of-the-customs-broker-regulations)). HQ 113965 (1997) put the same idea more tentatively: a flat amount "not tied to any particular transaction, would not trigger the regulation" ([HQ 113965](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/113965)). In January 2026, H350722 applied the rule to an online platform that connects importers to brokers. CBP said unlicensed entities "may not receive fees or other compensation such as a commission from a broker if such benefits are tied to particular customs business transactions." It also warned about the platform's own fee to importers: it "may violate 19 C.F.R. § 111.36(b) if the fee paid to the Unlicensed Company by an importer seeking to engage a third-party broker is tied to the entry filed by the broker" ([HQ H350722](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/H350722)).

That last warning reaches a model many refund firms use: charging the importer a percentage of refunds the broker recovers. HQ 113715 describes an older CBP decision (HQ 221330, 1991). There, a consultant who found drawback eligibility billed the importer, and the broker billed the consultant. CBP found a violation because the consultant "would receive a monetary benefit from the performance of Customs business by the broker for a third party" ([HQ 113715](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/113715)). No ruling addresses a refund-contingent fee that a software company charges the importer directly while the broker files. By the logic of H350722 and 221330, however, that fee is "tied to" the broker's filing, so treat it as high-risk.

Three arrangements are clearly safe. **Unpaid referrals** are fine: "gratuitous, informal business referrals to the brokerage firm do not constitute the 'agreement'" the rule targets (113715), and DuPont "may refer importers to DIO [a licensed broker], [but] it may not benefit from the services rendered as a result of the referral" (114404). **Owning shares in a brokerage** and taking ordinary dividends, with no per-referral payments, passed in 113715's second scenario. Any holder of 5% or more becomes a "principal" requiring notice and possible background checks under [19 CFR 111.28(e)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-111/subpart-C/section-111.28). **A flat, non-transactional fee** from a broker, such as a fixed monthly listing or sponsorship fee, matches CBP's stated dicta, though no ruling approves one on specific facts. One broker's public program pays CPAs and lawyers referral compensation "when Freight Right's fee is collected" ([Freight Right](https://www.freightright.com/ieepa-tariff-refund-partner-program)). That sits in visible tension with H276784, so it is not a model to copy. Protests add a narrow wrinkle. If protests are not customs business for an unlicensed agent (H272798), a broker's protest-only fee might fall outside 111.36(b). No ruling says so, and the conservative assumption is that the rule applies.

Attorney partnerships carry their own fee limits. A broker may not take from an attorney any fee "in excess of an amount measured by or commensurate with the time, effort and skill expended by the broker" ([19 CFR 111.35](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-111/subpart-C/section-111.35)). Lawyers are also bound by state versions of ABA Model Rule 5.4(a), which bars sharing legal fees with nonlawyers, and Rule 7.2(b), which bars paying for recommendations. The notes could not fetch the ABA text, so verify both. Washington's bar allows paying a lead generator only if it does not "recommend, endorse or vouch for" the lawyer and matches clients on objective criteria, much like a directory ([WSBA AO 201401](https://ao.wsba.org/print.aspx?ID=1680)). Finally, HTS Hero should not be named as the importer's refund recipient on Form 4811 or under 174.13(c). The Anti-Assignment Act, 31 U.S.C. 3727, covers "the authorization to receive payment for any part of the claim" ([Orrick](https://www.orrick.com/en/insights/2026/03/tariff-refund-claims-and-the-anti-assignment-of-claims-act)). No ruling decides whether CBP's payee-designation forms waive the Act.

## Four legitimate routes, with very different lead times

**Becoming a broker** unlocks everything but cannot happen quickly. The license exam is held on "the fourth Wednesday of April and October." Registration for Oct. 28, 2026 closed Sept. 16, so the **earliest attempt is April 2027**. Recent pass rates were 22% (April 2026), 12% (October 2025) and 30% (April 2025), and passing requires 75% ([CBP CBLE notice](https://www.cbp.gov/trade/programs-administration/customs-brokers/license-examination-notice-examination); [19 CFR 111.11](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/19/111.11)). CBP fees are modest: $390 for the exam, $300 for an individual license, $100 for the permit application and $190.88 a year for the permit ([CBP broker fees](https://www.cbp.gov/trade/programs-administration/customs-brokers/fees)), about $991 in total. After passing, CBP sends application instructions about 60 days later, followed by a multi-agency background check ([CBP Pub. 3001-1222](https://www.cbp.gov/sites/default/files/assets/documents/2022-Dec/CBP%20Publication%20%233001-1222%20-%20Customs%20Broker%20Licensing%20Process.pdf)). My estimate is a license in late 2027 at the earliest, and later if the first attempt fails. A license alone does not enable filing. PSCs and drawback also need ABI connectivity and a filer code, and drawback needs a bond. A **corporate license** is faster if HTS Hero can recruit a licensed broker as a real officer. 1641(b)(3) requires at least one licensed officer, and the license is revoked if none is in place for 120 days ([19 U.S.C. 1641](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/19/1641)). The officer must exercise "responsible supervision and control" (111.28), so a paid figurehead does not qualify. Once licensed, 111.36(b) no longer applies, because no unlicensed person is involved (cf. [HQ 116443](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/116443)).

**Partnering with a broker** is the fastest compliant route, but only one structure fits the rulings. The broker must execute its power of attorney "directly with the importer of record or drawback claimant, and not via a freight forwarder or other third party" (111.36(c)(3)). H350722 applies this to platforms: the unlicensed company "may not act as an intermediary between a broker and importer in the formation or execution of a POA." The broker, not HTS Hero's logic, must make every filing decision. H350722 says "A tool does not constitute a 'person' ... the actual decision regarding the classification of imported merchandise, or any other information needed to make entry, must be made by a duly licensed customs broker." The broker may share client records with HTS Hero only with written consent under [19 CFR 111.24](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-111/subpart-C/section-111.24). A general Form 5291 power of attorney "is insufficient to authorize sharing" ([HQ H318461](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/H318461)). An importer uploading its own records to HTS Hero, by contrast, is outside 111.24 (H350722). A fully white-labeled service, in which the importer sees only HTS Hero's brand "filing," runs into 111.37: "A broker must not allow his license, permit or name to be used by or for any unlicensed person." It also runs into 111.30(c)'s requirement of approval for trade names ([19 CFR 111.37](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-111/subpart-C/section-111.37)). One market example shows the shape: GingerControl says "We are not a customs broker, do not file your entries," and that "our licensed refund partner files it" ([GingerControl](https://www.gingercontrol.com/blog/ieepa-tariff-refund-guide)). Whether its partner is paid lawfully is not disclosed.

**Partnering with a customs attorney** covers protests (attorneys need no power of attorney under 174.3(a)(1)) and all CIT litigation. It does not cover CAPE or, on the notes' reading, PSCs or drawback, because no attorney exemption from 1641 exists. The attorney must be engaged and paid by the importer, and HTS Hero cannot take a share of the fee.

**Supporting importer self-filing with software** fits CAPE and protests best. Neither needs ABI: CAPE is a CSV upload in the ACE Portal, and ACE offers a standalone protest account ([FR 2016-14003](https://www.gpo.gov/fdsys/pkg/FR-2016-06-14/html/2016-14003.htm)). Here the notes conflict a second time. The licensing note said "A software company can legally prepare files for the importer to submit itself." That cannot be squared with 114199's "The fact that the client will sign and file them is immaterial," or with H350722's holding that a tool's output counts as the decision of the company behind it. On CBP's text, the defensible line is software the importer drives. Under that line, the importer chooses which entries to claim, and the tool formats the importer's own choices. Software that decides which entries qualify and produces a ready-to-upload CAPE file is what H350722 condemned. No ruling addresses CAPE tools, so this is an inference. Whatever the route, **HTS Hero can always charge the importer directly for its own non-customs-business product**, such as a subscription for analysis, education or monitoring, priced independently of any entry, refund or filing (113715, H350722). For certainty on any specific feature, 19 CFR Part 177 allows a binding ruling request. That is how every company in these rulings got its answer.

## Mapping HTS Hero's features onto the line

CBP's working test across the rulings separates **general information** from **entry-specific determinations that will or may end up on a filing**. HQ 114404 gives the permitted side: "an unlicensed person could explain to a third party the use of the Harmonized Tariff Schedules, the General Rules of Interpretation, and the Explanatory Notes ... That same person would be precluded, however, from classifying a client's merchandise that is going to be the subject of an entry filed with Customs." For past entries, HQ H175280 (Sept. 24, 2012) approved software that compared filed entry data with source documents "to determine if there are discrepancies with regard to value, classification, and quantity." The discrepancies went to a licensed broker "for review and resolution." CBP held "the identification of discrepancies between entry documents filed with CBP and clients' records is not customs business" ([HQ H175280](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/H175280)). H272798 marks the far boundary: "If, however, 'identifying duty recovery opportunities' requires Company A to make decisions with regard to value, classification, and quantity entered, or resolving discrepancies or irregularities in CBP transactions, such activities constitute 'customs business'." HQ 115248 adds that duty verification is broker work "when the possibility exists that corrected duty information derived from the duty verification process will ultimately appear on the entry." A tension remains unresolved. 114199's 1998 line barred advice on "particular entries or drawback claims filed or to be filed," which is broader than the later audit rulings allow. CBP has never reconciled the two, and both remain published.

| Underpayment flags | H272798: unlicensed filing of prior disclosures is allowed "if given proper authorization," because they "relate to transactions that are already completed" | Permitted, including preparing and filing a prior disclosure with authorization | None identified in the rulings |
| Savings and trade-program screening | No ruling on point; 114404, 114654 general-vs-specific line | Permitted as general eligibility ("goods of code X from country Y may qualify for Z if conditions are met") | Deciding that a specific entry qualifies and supplying the SPI or claim data to file ("cannot decide what data should appear on an entry," H350722) |

| Managed: protests for clients | 114199, 114404, 115248, H272798 | Permitted with a specific 174.3 power of attorney, at a fee not tied to a broker's filing | Appearing alone before CBP; drafting legal arguments may raise state UPL issues (unresearched); any CIT step |

The $1,200-a-month Managed tier described in recent commits fits the fee rules as a flat subscription the importer pays HTS Hero. It stops fitting if part of it pays a broker for filings, or if it scales with refunds a broker recovers (H350722, 221330). Marketing copy matters too. A broker must not "knowingly use false or misleading representations to procure employment" ([19 CFR 111.38](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-111/subpart-C/section-111.38)), so any co-marketing should keep the project's "could save / may qualify" phrasing.

## Questions to put to a customs attorney before selling a filing service

The notes leave several questions that no CBP text resolves. Each one is a specific question a customs attorney can answer, or that a Part 177 ruling request could settle.

## Conclusion

The earlier answers were wrong in both directions. One said a power of attorney lets HTS Hero file anything. The other said HTS Hero can't touch protests. CBP's position is narrower and more useful than either: **protests are the one filing an unlicensed agent may prepare, sign and file for pay, and every other refund instrument is broker work no matter who presses submit**. For HTS Hero, this means the product's legal risk is concentrated in what Managed promises, more than in the refund question. "Audit every entry" describes the H175280 model CBP approved. "Keep imports in the lowest-duty position" describes the 114404 activity CBP prohibited, and the classification assistant may already sit in H290535 territory regardless of refunds. Rewording Managed as monitoring, flagging and protest filing, with all prospective decisions left to the importer's broker, is a copy change. It would move the service onto ground CBP has approved.

The money question has a cleaner answer than expected. HTS Hero earns from importers, at prices it sets independently of what any broker files. Brokers can pay it nothing per client, at most a flat fee. The protest exception, used deliberately for ordinary disputes over classification and rates and for Section 301 exclusion refunds, is the only filing revenue available before a license. That makes the April 2027 broker exam, or recruiting a licensed officer, a strategic decision rather than a formality: it is the only route to PSC, drawback and CAPE work. A Part 177 ruling request on the Tracker audit and the Managed workflow is inexpensive by comparison. Every company in these rulings got its certainty that way.
