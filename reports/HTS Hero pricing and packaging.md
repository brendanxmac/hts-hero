# Charge for watching imports, not lookups

HTS Hero's three access levels (anonymous visitor, free account, paid Tracker) are the right skeleton, but the paid level should split into two feature tiers plus a multi-client plan, and the free account should keep a small number of tracked imports instead of none. The recommended lineup has five price points. The calculator stays free with no account. A free account gets the research layer plus 3 tracked imports on email alerts. **Alerts** costs $19–$149/mo for 10–500 imports. **Pro** costs $39–$299/mo for the same steps and adds savings checks, entry audits, Slack/Teams and seats. **Broker & Forwarder** starts at $399/mo for 1,000 pooled imports across client workspaces. **Managed** stays from $1,200/mo. The value metric throughout is the import (one HTS code from one country of origin), and the 14-day, 200-import, no-card reverse trial stays as the main conversion engine. Of the founder's two ideas, the $9 advanced-calculator plan should be dropped: duty calculators are free across the market, HTS Hero already retired a $14.99 calculator plan on Oct 10, and expert guidance runs against cheap single-user plans in B2B. The alerts-only base offer is right, and it is essentially the Alerts tier above, priced close to the only published comparable (TariffDesk, $29 for 10 codes). Treat every number here as a hypothesis. HTS Hero has no conversion, revenue or churn data yet, and its pricing has changed five times in eleven days. The biggest constraint on revenue is not price but the license line: HTS Hero can find, flag and explain, but it cannot file refunds, take success fees, or classify a user's specific imports to 10 digits.

## Three levels work, but a free account with no tracked imports has nothing to come back to

The current structure follows one rule: "Looking up a tariff is free. Watching, auditing and saving on your own products is paid" ([PRICING.md](../PRICING.md)). Anonymous visitors get the full calculator, Duty over time, side-by-side country compare and Tariff News subscriptions. A free account adds the plain-English "why", legal text, sourcing intelligence, past-date duty and an unlimited catalog with rate history and export. Since Oct 18, though, the free plan tracks **zero imports** once the trial ends. It gets no alerts, no impact checks and no AD/CVD updates on its own products (`alertImports = 0` in [libs/tariff-tracker/plan.ts](../libs/tariff-tracker/plan.ts); [PRICING.md](../PRICING.md)). The access matrix itself is sound. The market expects a duty estimate to be free: Flexport, Tariffs API, AMZ Prep, Gateway Lines and government lookups charge nothing ([Flexport via BusinessWire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20260120812295/en/Flexport-Launches-Tariff-Refund-Calculator-Giving-Businesses-Early-Insight-Into-Possible-Tariff-Refunds); [Tariffs API](https://tariffsapi.com/tariff-calculator?hts=9615)). And email capture is the calculator's real job. Freemium products also tend to run four public tiers, counting free; 67% of four-tier pricing pages include a free plan ([SaaS Research Lab](https://saasresearchlab.com/blog/4-tiers-pricing)).

The weak spot is the zero allowance. Every monitoring product found in the research includes alerts on its free tier and rations them by volume, speed or channel rather than removing them. Visualping's free plan watches 5 pages with email alerts, Distill's watches 25 monitors, and UptimeRobot's free plan has *more* monitors than its $10 Solo plan ([Visualping](https://visualping.io/pricing); [Distill](https://distill.io/pricing/); [UptimeRobot](https://uptimerobot.com/pricing/)). The direct tariff competitors do the same: TariffClassify gives one free watch and US Tariff Rates gives **5 free watchlist alerts** ([TariffClassify](https://tariffclassify.ai/tariff-rate-alerts); [US Tariff Rates](https://ustariffrates.com/pricing)). A free account with three tracked imports gets an email whenever a tariff action hits one of them. Each email is a reason to log in, and it pairs naturally with HTS Hero's existing rule that impact checks show untracked imports as a count: "It also hits N imports you're not tracking" ([MVP_TRIM.md](../MVP_TRIM.md)). That line is the upgrade prompt, and it only fires for people who still receive alerts.

The best case for zero is that loss aversion works hardest when the free plan has nothing to lose into, and that is the stated reason for the reverse trial ([reverse-trial memory](file:///Users/brendanmclaughlin/.claude/projects/-Users-brendanmclaughlin-Projects-HTS-Hero-hts-hero/memory/reverse-trial.md)). The counter-argument is that the importers three free alerts would fully satisfy have five or fewer pairs and would rarely pay anyway. Everyone else feels the gap on every alert. Reverse-trial practitioners frame the model as capturing trial-level conversion *while keeping an engaged free base* ([Elena Verna, Amplitude](https://amplitude.com/blog/reverse-trial)). A free plan with no recurring touchpoint keeps no base. The decision is cheap to reverse and should be tested. The recommendation is to ship 3 free tracked imports, email only, and compare 30-day trial-to-paid against a zero-import cohort before settling.

Keep the reverse trial. The only large benchmark (OpenView, Growth Unhinged and Pendo, 1,000+ products) puts "good" self-serve freemium conversion at **3–5%** and "good" free-trial conversion at **8–12%** ([Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/what-is-a-good-free-to-paid-conversion)). Verna estimates about 15% of no-card trial starts convert ([Amplitude](https://amplitude.com/blog/reverse-trial)). Airtable's reported 40% lift from a reverse trial is a headline claim, not a controlled study ([Failory](https://newsletter.failory.com/p/no-freemium)). Starting the trial at the first real upload rather than at sign-up was the right call. It means the trial clock measures product use, not inbox neglect.

## A $9 calculator plan would sell what competitors give away

The founder's idea (a) is a ~$9/mo "advanced calculator" with full history and extras, leaving the higher-value features to the Tracker. It fails on three pieces of evidence. First, HTS Hero has already run this experiment: it removed a **$14.99/mo Tariff Calculator plan** and the 20-calculation monthly free limit on Oct 10, 2026 ([PRICING.md](../PRICING.md)). A third-party comparison still lists HTS Hero at "$14.99/mo unlimited calculations", so the stale offer is still circulating ([ustariffrates comparison](https://ustariffrates.com/best/best-trade-compliance-software)). Second, the only paid calculator access left in the market is metered API usage (SimplyDuty £9.99 for 100 calls, about £0.10 per call) or a calculator bundled into $99–$1,399/mo classification suites ([SimplyDuty](https://www.simplyduty.com/pricing/); [Gaia Dynamics](https://gaiadynamics.ai/pricing)). Nobody sells a standalone premium calculator to US importers at $9, because Flexport gives a better-known one away. Third, Kyle Poyar's PLG guidance says single-user plans have lower retention, lower LTV:CAC and a heavier support burden. Notion dropped its Personal Pro plan and **doubled its entry price from $4 to $8** ([Growth Unhinged](https://www.growthunhinged.com/p/your-guide-to-plg-pricing-201)).

The idea also has a structural problem specific to HTS Hero. Its obvious contents (past-date duty, the "why", legal text, sourcing) are what the free account currently uses to win email sign-ups ([ConversionDialog.tsx](../components/duty-calculator/calculator/ConversionDialog.tsx)). Moving them behind $9 weakens the sign-up that feeds the Tracker funnel. Leaving them free leaves the $9 plan nothing to sell. A $9 plan also invites buy-down. A user who would have started the 200-import trial may settle for the calculator plan and never upload imports, the activation event the whole funnel depends on. Steven Forth warns that an over-featured lower tier removes the reason to upgrade ([Pricing Innovation](https://pricinginnovation.substack.com/p/generative-ai-has-over-indexed-on)). No quantitative study of sub-$10 tiers was found, so this risk is reasoned, not measured.

Cheap tiers do work when they are a smaller slice of the same value metric. UptimeRobot Solo ($9–10 for 10 monitors), Visualping Personal ($10–14 for 10 pages) and Distill Starter ($15 for 50 monitors) are all fewer units of the main product, not a different product ([UptimeRobot](https://uptimerobot.com/pricing/); [Visualping](https://visualping.io/pricing); [Distill](https://distill.io/pricing/)). So if the founder wants a price near $9, it belongs at the bottom of the import ladder, not on the calculator. A practical variant: offer Alerts for 10 imports at **$15/mo billed annually** ($19 monthly). That reaches the $9–15 psychological band without creating a second product line.

## Alerts-only is the right base plan, and it is priced below the market

The founder's idea (b), a base offer of just tariff alerts for a set number of imports, is well supported. In monitoring products, alerts *are* the value metric. Few tariff vendors sell alerts matched to specific HTS codes, and those that do meter by codes watched, not seats ([GingerControl comparison](https://gingercontrol.com/blog/trade-policy-alert-services-compared-2026)). The closest published ladder is **TariffDesk: $29/mo for 10 codes, $59 for 50, $99 for 500** ([TariffDesk](https://tariffdesk.com/)). US Tariff Rates Pro is $49/mo and Team $199/mo. GingerControl's product-matched Compliance Radar sits inside its $150/mo GingerLite credits ([US Tariff Rates](https://ustariffrates.com/pricing); [GingerControl pricing](https://gingercontrol.com/pricing)). HTS Hero's unit is finer than most. It keys each alert on code *plus country of origin*, which matters because Section 301, 232 and 122 measures stack differently by origin. Only TariffClassify explicitly ties origin to a watch ([TariffClassify](https://tariffclassify.ai/tariff-rate-alerts)). HTS Hero also says what changed: rates before → after, +X%, with the legal source.

Against that market, the live launch prices of **$12 / $29 / $89 / $149 for 10 / 30 / 100 / 500 imports** ([pricing.ts](../components/pricing-calculator/lib/pricing.ts)) are low at the entry and roughly at market at the top. TariffDesk charges $29 for 10 codes where HTS Hero charges $12. The SaaS median starter price across 110 pricing pages is $29 ([SaaS Research Lab](https://saasresearchlab.com/blog/4-tiers-pricing)). For a small importer, the anchor is the broker's fee of **$100–$250 per entry** ([Strix](https://strixsmart.com/resources/blog/customs-broker-cost)). A $19–$39 monthly plan costs less than one entry, and pain recurs with every new action. Five or more regime changes have hit importers in 18 months: the end of de minimis, the IEEPA ruling, Section 122, and new Section 301 tariffs from July 23, 2026 ([Tradlinx](https://blogs.tradlinx.com/section-301-replaced-the-global-tariff-here-is-how-the-new-system-works/); [EY](https://taxnews.ey.com/news/2026-0486-us-supreme-court-rules-ieepa-does-not-authorize-presidents-to-impose-tariffs)). Poyar's broader finding that founders underprice by projecting their own price sensitivity points the same way ([SaaS Club](https://saasclub.io/podcast/saas-pricing-strategy-mistakes-kyle-poyar/)).

The recommended Alerts ladder is **$19 / $39 / $89 / $149 for 10 / 30 / 100 / 500 imports**. That raises only the two bottom steps, keeps the top steps where TariffDesk's 500-code plan and US Tariff Rates' $199 Team plan bracket them, and keeps a per-import fall from $1.90 to $0.30. That fall of about 84% sits inside the 50–90% range seen across Prisync, UptimeRobot and Distill ([Prisync](https://prisync.com/pricing/)). It also keeps the price step between tiers near the 2–2.5x pattern those analogs use. Alerts includes everything the Tracker MVP sells today: alerts on tracked imports (email and in-app), AD/CVD alerts, impact checks, Insights, Tariff News, rate history and export ([features.ts](../components/tariff-tracker/access/features.ts)). It does not include savings, audits, Slack/Teams delivery or extra seats.

## Savings, audits and multi-client work earn their own tiers

Savings detection and entry auditing tie most directly to money, so they belong in a premium tier rather than buried in every plan. Two things push this way. Poyar's 2026 monetization survey finds premium editions typically priced **50–100% above the base**, and hybrid pricing is rising (37%, from 25% a year earlier) ([Growth Unhinged 2026](https://www.growthunhinged.com/p/the-state-of-b2b-monetization-in-2026)). Monitoring analogs gate team and integration features, not core units, at the business tier. Visualping keeps the same per-check price on Business but adds Slack/Teams, API, bulk import and seats ([Visualping](https://visualping.io/pricing)). Internally, the docs disagree on this. MVP_TRIM.md and the `features.ts` header say savings and audits return "as their own subscription", while PRICING.md and the marketing constants promise they are "included in the trial and every paid Tracker plan at no extra cost" ([MVP_TRIM.md](../MVP_TRIM.md); [comingSoon.ts](../components/ui/marketing/comingSoon.ts)). Settle that by honoring the promise for launch subscribers and splitting the tiers for everyone after.

Annual billing should come back at **"2 months free" (about 17%)**, in line with the 10–20% analog range and ProfitWell's reported ~16% average annual discount. ProfitWell also reported lower churn where most contracts are annual: about 5% versus about 9% with none (secondary and older) ([Monetizely](https://www.getmonetizely.com/articles/why-annual-billing-discounts-work-better-than-you-think-a-revenue-game-changer-for-saas)). Anyone who subscribes before the Fri Nov 13 launch deadline keeps their price for life, as already promised ([PRICING.md](../PRICING.md)). They should also get Pro features when savings and audits ship, labeled a "Founding" plan. That keeps the marketing promise and gives the countdown a real reason to exist. New subscribers after Nov 13 see Alerts and Pro. Pro's prices sit near the "next prices" the code already holds ($19 / $49 / $149 / $249), which were meant to include savings and audits ([pricing.ts](../components/pricing-calculator/lib/pricing.ts)). The recommendation simply makes the inclusion explicit and separable.

The Broker & Forwarder plan fills the biggest hole in the current lineup: nothing sits between $149/mo for 500 imports and Managed at $1,200/mo. The multi-client market is about **6,200 brokerage firms and 14,500 licensed brokers** ([CBP](https://www.cbp.gov/node/77830)), plus roughly 3,300 ocean forwarders and 9,000 NVOCCs on the FMC register ([Causo guide](https://hub.causo.ai/guides/freight-forwarder-list-2026)). Their clients ask "what will this cost me?" after every action. The research found no self-serve, published-price broker plan. Analog multi-client pricing runs per client or per workspace with unlimited users: Pixie charges about $65–$129/mo by client count, and SE Ranking sells a +$69/mo Agency Pack ([ZoftwareHub](https://zoftwarehub.com/en-ae/products/pixie/pricing); [SE Ranking](https://seranking.com/subscription.html)). Pooling imports across clients keeps one value metric. The published "from" price follows Visualping's "Solutions from $3,000/yr" anchor and fits HTS Hero's no-demo positioning, while still allowing a call. This plan is the least validated in the lineup. It needs client workspaces that don't exist yet, and brokers may expect pilots, as Cervo (now Altana) offers ([usecervo.com](https://usecervo.com/)). Build it only after 10 broker conversations confirm the shape.

Two smaller offers round this out. A **flat-fee one-time Entry Audit** for importers who won't subscribe gives the /tariff-audit lead form a paid path, at about one month of a mid-tier plan ($149–$249). The research found no comparable price, so this is a guess to test. It must cover only entries since **April 8, 2026**, where verified historical rates begin ([htsHero.ts](../libs/compare/tools/htsHero.ts)). A **rate API plus change feed** at roughly $49–$199/mo would sit inside the observed developer band ([US Tariff Rates developers](https://ustariffrates.com/developers); [Zonos docs](https://docs.zonos.com/catalog/classify/pricing)). It should wait: free tariff MCPs are becoming table stakes, and no evidence of willingness to pay for them was found ([Gateway Lines](https://gatewaylines.com/press-releases/your-ai-can-now-price-us-import-tariffs)).

## The license line caps refund upside and threatens classification

The largest pool of money in this market is refunds, and HTS Hero can capture little of it directly. About **$166B** of IEEPA duties were paid by over 330,000 importers. As of Oct 2, 2026, CBP had already accepted about **$136.6B** in refunds through CAPE, its free filing module, and Phase 3 (finally liquidated entries) covers only about $11.4B ([Supply Chain Dive](https://www.supplychaindive.com/news/cbp-starts-paying-out-ieepa-tariff-refunds-for-finally-liquidated-entries/832411/)). Only the importer of record or its licensed broker can file ([White and Williams](https://www.whiteandwilliams.com/resources-alerts-IEEPA-Tariff-Refunds-CBP-Launches-CAPE-Process)). Recovery firms charge contingency: 15–25% for IEEPA work, and from "as low as 1.5%" at Tariff Recovery Group ([Ayar Law](https://ayarlaw.com/tariff-refund-attorney); [Tariff Recovery Group](https://tariffrecoverygroup.com/)). HTS Hero cannot copy that model. 19 USC 1641 defines customs business to include "the refund, rebate, or drawback" of duties and the preparation of documents meant for filing, whether or not the preparer files them. HTS Hero's own legal rules already bar success fees on broker-filed refunds and broker referral fees or revenue sharing under 19 CFR 111.36(b) ([CBP ruling H350722](https://rulings.cbp.gov/api/getdoc/hq/2026/H350722.pdf); [unlicensed memory note](file:///Users/brendanmclaughlin/.claude/projects/-Users-brendanmclaughlin-Projects-HTS-Hero-hts-hero/memory/unlicensed-find-flag-explain.md)). That also rules out one idea in the partner research: collecting a referral bounty from brokers for flagged leads. Most IEEPA entries also predate HTS Hero's verified rate history (from April 2026), so an IEEPA refund product would be a shrinking, legally contingent line. Audits should be priced as subscription value in Pro or as a flat one-time fee, never as a share of recovered duty.

CBP ruling **HQ H350722** (2026) also draws lines that touch the product directly. AI classification suggestions to 6 digits are not customs business. Suggestions beyond 6 digits for goods that are or will be imported "generally" require a license, and **a disclaimer does not cure this**. A general classification database is "narrowly permissible" only with a meaningfully implemented disclaimer and no link to a specific entry. Extracting entry data from shipping documents counts as preparing part of an entry even if nothing is filed ([CBP ruling H350722](https://rulings.cbp.gov/api/getdoc/hq/2026/H350722.pdf)). Three consequences follow for packaging. HTS Hero still sells Classification at $39 / $89 / $429 ([pricing.ts](../components/pricing-calculator/lib/pricing.ts)), and the business already moved away from it because of this ruling. It should be sunset, reframed to 6-digit or general research, or run with a licensed broker in the loop, and customs counsel should review it before more money goes into it. Any feature that auto-assigns 10-digit codes to a user's catalog (a Shopify catalog import, for example) carries the same risk. And savings checks must say an import "may qualify" for USMCA or an exclusion, never decide that it does. Audits must flag potential over- or underpayment on the code as entered for the broker to act on, which HQ H175280 permits ([unlicensed memory note](file:///Users/brendanmclaughlin/.claude/projects/-Users-brendanmclaughlin-Projects-HTS-Hero-hts-hero/memory/unlicensed-find-flag-explain.md)). The Broker & Forwarder plan helps here strategically: when a licensed broker is the customer, the broker makes the filing decisions.

Other risks are commercial. Raising the 10-import price from $12 to $19 may lower sign-up-to-paid among micro importers, the group the 3 free imports are meant to keep engaged. The "Coming Oct 2026" label on savings and audits leaves only Oct 22–31 to ship. If either slips, the label breaks a public promise in days ([PRE_DEPLOY.md](../PRE_DEPLOY.md)). Stale HTS Hero prices are already circulating in competitor comparisons (GingerControl lists $49 / $79 / $149), which confuses buyers ([GingerControl](https://gingercontrol.com/blog/trade-policy-alert-services-compared-2026)). The committed homepage FAQ promises certain refunds, cites audits that aren't live, and renders "Alerts are free on 0 imports" into the FAQPage structured data ([copy.ts](../components/home/lib/copy.ts)).

## Conclusion

The question was framed as "how many user levels", but the evidence says the levels matter less than what each level *watches*. HTS Hero's free tools match the market, and its paid value is change detection on a customer's own code-and-origin pairs. The structure should therefore give people a small, real taste of watching for free, charge a market price for watching at scale, and charge a premium for the work that turns watching into money (savings, audits, teams and many clients). The tools for a disciplined launch are already in the product. The upload-triggered reverse trial, the "hits N imports you're not tracking" count and the per-import price display are exactly the mechanics the analogs use. What's missing is measurement, so the most valuable pricing move this month is to stop moving prices.

The deeper implication is strategic. Because the license line forbids success fees and filing, HTS Hero cannot win the refund gold rush the way law firms and contingency recovery firms do. Its durable advantage is recurring: tariff actions now arrive every few weeks, and every one is a purchase trigger for an origin-aware monitoring tool. That favors subscriptions over one-time products, broker channels over direct refund services, and a premium Pro tier over a cheaper calculator. The upside sits above $149/mo, with brokers and mid-market teams, not below $12.
