# ICPs and Willingness to Pay for a Self-Serve US Duty/Tariff SaaS (HTS Hero)

Research date: 2026-10-21. US-focused. Every item under "Cited Findings" has an inline source. "Inferences" are my reasoning, not sourced fact. Many pricing and fee sources are vendor marketing pages, and they are flagged where that applies.

Product capabilities assessed: (a) advanced duty calculator, (b) tariff-change alerts on the customer's own imports, (c) impact / landed-cost insight, (d) savings detection (USMCA, exclusions, lower-duty origins), (e) entry audits / overpayment and refund checks. The Tracker is priced by number of imports (HTS + country-of-origin pairs).

---

## 1. Market size: how many US importers, how are they distributed, and how many brokers and forwarders?

### Takeaway
There are about 240,000 identified US importing firms, and about 97% are small or medium-sized (under 500 employees). Large firms carry most of the import value. CBP's IEEPA refund figure counts more than 330,000 importers of record, which is a wider count than the Census firm count. The service side adds about 14,500 licensed individual customs brokers, roughly 3,300 licensed ocean forwarders, and roughly 9,000 NVOCCs.

### Cited Findings
- 2024 (preliminary): 239,231 identified US importers accounting for $2,921B of $3,266B total goods imports. Of these, 41,531 were manufacturers and 87,429 wholesalers — [Census 2024 preliminary profile](https://www.census.gov/foreign-trade/Press-Release/edb/2024prelimprofile.pdf)
- 2023 (final): 242,515 identified importers (42,223 manufacturing, 89,233 wholesale), about $2.74T known import value — [Census 2024 preliminary profile](https://www.census.gov/foreign-trade/Press-Release/edb/2024prelimprofile.pdf)
- 2022: 234,074 identified importers (+3.3% vs 2021). 17.8% were manufacturers, 37.5% wholesalers, and 44.7% "other" (retail, e-commerce, services and so on). Large importers held nearly two-thirds of known import value — [Census Profile 2021–2022](https://www.census.gov/foreign-trade/Press-Release/edb/edbrel2022.pdf)
- Census defines SMEs as firms with fewer than 500 employees, plus nonemployers and firms with missing employment data. In 2015, SMEs were 97% of goods importers. SMEs account for only about a third of goods trade value (this figure covers all goods trade, not imports alone) — [Census 2015 preliminary profile](https://www.census.gov/foreign-trade/Press-Release/edb/2015prelimprofile.pdf); [trade.gov SME article](https://trade.gov/feature-article/small-and-medium-sized-enterprises-reaching-new-markets)
- In an earlier Census release, "small" meant 99 employees or fewer — [Census Profile 2021–2022](https://www.census.gov/foreign-trade/Press-Release/edb/edbrel2022.pdf)
- IEEPA refund universe: about $166B across about 53M entries from more than 330,000 importers, per a CBP collection figure cited by Kilpatrick — [Tradlinx summary](https://blogs.tradlinx.com/ieepa-tariff-refunds-where-the-166-billion-refund-process-actually-stands-and-what-importers-should-do-now/); [GHY CAPE update](https://www.ghy.com/trade-compliance/cbp-cape-ieepa-refund-progress/)
- Customs brokers: about 14,454 active licensed individual brokers, per a CBP page last modified around March 2024. A secondary 2026 source cites about 14,500 individual brokers and about 6,200 licensed brokerage firms. An older figure of about 11,000 (J.J. Keller) is outdated — [CBP](https://www.cbp.gov/node/77830); [J.J. Keller](https://jjkellercompliancenetwork.com/regsense/customs-brokers)
- Forwarders/NVOCCs: one secondary guide reports, from the FMC register in August 2026, 3,272 active licensed ocean freight forwarders and 9,049 active licensed or registered NVOCCs. These may double-count firms that hold both licenses. The FMC itself says more than 9,000 OTIs are licensed or registered (domestic plus foreign), including 5,119 active domestically licensed OTIs (undated) — [Causo guide](https://hub.causo.ai/guides/freight-forwarder-list-2026); [FMC](https://www.fmc.gov/?p=6327)
- Before August 29, 2025, more than 1.36B de minimis shipments entered the US in one fiscal year. Those shippers now need entries — [AOL/AP](https://www.aol.com/small-businesses-scrambling-us-tariff-164306069.html)
- Small Business Majority's July 2025 survey: about a quarter of small businesses import directly, and 45% buy from US suppliers who import — [Small Business Majority 2026 fact sheet](https://smallbusinessmajority.org/sites/default/files/policy-docs/2026-tariffs-fact-sheet.pdf)

### Inferences
- The difference between the Census firm count (about 240K) and CBP's IOR count (more than 330K) probably reflects foreign IORs, multiple IOR numbers per firm, non-resident e-commerce sellers, and individuals. Census also excludes firms it cannot link to the Business Register. For TAM, the reasonable range is about 240K US firms to about 330K or more IORs.
- Rough segment counts (my estimate, built on Census shares):
  - Micro/small (under 100 employees): likely about 200K or more firms. This includes most of the 44.7% "other" bucket where DTC and e-commerce sellers sit.
  - Mid-market (100–499 employees): roughly 15–25K.
  - Large/enterprise (500 or more): about 7K (3% of 240K).
- Service providers who could resell or serve many clients: about 6,200 brokerage firms and about 3–5K domestic forwarders/OTIs. Together that is roughly 10K multi-client organizations.
- Since de minimis ended, many former de minimis shippers (cross-border DTC brands, including foreign sellers) have become new or occasional entry filers. That is a new pool of low-sophistication importers. I found no confirmed CBP data on how many new IORs this created.

### Gaps
- The final Census 2023–2024 profile (released May 5, 2026) has exact size-class tables (counts and import value by employment band), but I could not extract text from the PDF. Next step: open https://www.census.gov/foreign-trade/Press-Release/edb/edbrel2024.pdf manually, Exhibit 1b / Exhibit 4.
- There is no official 2025–2026 CBP broker count, and no count of active IOR numbers filing in 2025–2026.
- There is no hard data on new IOR registrations after de minimis ended.

## 2. Pain since the 2025–2026 tariff upheaval, and what importers pay today

### Takeaway
Pain has been high and keeps recurring. There were five or more regime changes in 18 months: IEEPA, the end of de minimis in August 2025, IEEPA struck down in February 2026, the Section 122 global 10% from February to July 2026, and new Section 301 tariffs from July 23, 2026, along with Section 232 expansions. Each change is a purchase trigger. Today importers pay brokers about $100–$400 per entry, refund lawyers contingency fees (about 20% illustrative), and enterprises pay $200K or more per year for GTM suites. Between the free calculators and those suites there is a clear gap in price and sophistication.

### Cited Findings: timeline
- De minimis was suspended for all countries effective August 29, 2025 (EO 14324). Sellers who had never filed an entry must now file for goods up to $2,500. Etsy and Shopify sellers, subscription boxes, and drop shippers are the most exposed — [Thomson Reuters](https://www.thomsonreuters.com/en-us/posts/corporates/sunset-of-de-minimis/); [Easyship](https://www.easyship.com/blog/de-minimis-exemption-suspension)
- February 20, 2026: the Supreme Court in *Learning Resources v. Trump* held 6-3 that IEEPA does not authorize tariffs, and left refunds to the CIT — [EY](https://taxnews.ey.com/news/2026-0486-us-supreme-court-rules-ieepa-does-not-authorize-presidents-to-impose-tariffs); [Baker Tilly](https://www.bakertilly.com/insights/ieepa-tariff-refunds-and-the-supreme-court-ruling)
- A 10% global tariff under Section 122 took effect February 24, 2026, with a 150-day limit. The CIT ruled it unlawful on May 7, 2026, but relief applied only to the plaintiffs. The CAFC entered an administrative stay on May 12. The tariff expired July 24, 2026 — [Gibson Dunn](https://www.gibsondunn.com/section-122-global-tariffs-invalidated-by-the-court-of-international-trade-ruling-and-next-steps/); [Snell & Wilmer](https://www.swlaw.com/publication/more-tariff-refunds-not-so-fast-court-of-international-trade-strikes-down-section-122-tariffs-but-relief-is-limited-to-plaintiffs/); [Tradlinx](https://blogs.tradlinx.com/section-301-replaced-the-global-tariff-here-is-how-the-new-system-works/)
- New Section 301 tariffs of 10% or 12.5% on goods from 60 economies (forced-labor finding) followed final action on July 23, 2026. A separate excess-capacity Section 301 investigation of 16 economies is ongoing, and a Vietnam IP investigation opened May 29, 2026 — [Tradlinx](https://blogs.tradlinx.com/section-301-replaced-the-global-tariff-here-is-how-the-new-system-works/); [White & Case](https://www.whitecase.com/insight-alert/ustr-initiates-section-301-investigations-16-us-trade-partners-targeting-industrial); [Dorsey](https://www.dorsey.com/newsresources/publications/client-alerts/2026/6/new-section-301-tariffs)
- CAPE, CBP's refund portal, Phase 1 went live in ACE on April 20, 2026. Refunds are not automatic: importers must file declarations and keep ACH details current, and payments run about 60–90 days after acceptance. Phase 1 excludes AD/CVD, reconciliation, drawback, and protested entries. Fewer than 10% of eligible importers had enrolled in ACH as of late March 2026 — [GHY](https://www.ghy.com/trade-compliance/cbp-cape-ieepa-refund-progress/); [C.H. Robinson](https://www.chrobinson.com/resources/insights-and-advisories/client-advisories/2026q2/04-13-2026-client-advisory-cbp-announces-cape-tool-ieepa-duty-refund-claims/); [Tradlinx](https://blogs.tradlinx.com/ieepa-tariff-refunds-where-the-166-billion-refund-process-actually-stands-and-what-importers-should-do-now/)
- Troutman's October 2026 overview says CBP's administrative refunds for finally liquidated entries are limited to CIT plaintiffs with court-ordered reliquidation, so some entries still need litigation — [Troutman Oct 2026](https://www.troutman.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/Troutman-IEEPA-Tariff-Refund-Practice-Overview-October-2026.pdf)

### Cited Findings: magnitude of pain
- Small Business Majority, July 2025: 60% of small businesses reported higher costs from tariffs, and 57% said costs rose 10–25% — [Small Business Majority](https://smallbusinessmajority.org/sites/default/files/policy-docs/2026-tariffs-fact-sheet.pdf)
- Boston Fed: SMB-realized tariff rate went from about 6.5% (January 2025) to about 11.4% (July 2025). Importers expected to pass about two-thirds of the cost through to prices — [Boston Fed](https://www.bostonfed.org/publications/current-policy-perspectives/2025/smb-expectations-concerning-tariffs-costs-prices); [Boston Fed](https://www.bostonfed.org/publications/current-policy-perspectives/2025/who-pays-for-tariffs)
- Center for American Progress (an advocacy group), April 2026: small-business importers' tariff bills roughly tripled. Its estimate is about $306,000 average extra cost per small importer over the 12 months to February 2026 — [CAP](https://www.americanprogress.org/article/in-the-first-year-president-trumps-tariffs-have-cost-small-business-importers-306000-on-average/) (page returned 403, so I relied on the search summary)
- KPMG Tariff Pulse: 57% of US companies reported declining gross margins from tariffs, and firms are investing in automation and technology — [KPMG](https://kpmg.com/us/en/media/news/us-businesses-experiencing-impacts-from-tariffs.html)
- Thomson Reuters 2025 Tariffs Survey (285 trade professionals at companies with $200M or more in revenue): almost three-quarters are changing or considering changing sourcing — [Thomson Reuters](https://www.thomsonreuters.com/en/institute/reports/2025-tariffs-survey)
- RSM, more than 300 mid-sized supply-chain decision-makers: firms are putting money into technology, data, and compliance — [SupplyChain247](https://www.supplychain247.com/article/mid-market-companies-technology-offset-tariffs)
- Amazon/e-commerce: SmartScout found 930 items whose prices rose an average of 29% after April 9, 2025. DataWeave found 475 of 1,407 China-origin SKUs rose in price — [TipRanks](https://www.tipranks.com/news/amazon-amzn-sellers-scramble-to-stay-competitive-as-tariffs-force-higher-prices); [RetailWire](https://retailwire.com/amazon-chinese-sellers-raise-prices-tariffs/)
- User report of a surprise bill: a Shopify merchant saw a calculator apply 95–100% duty where they expected 3.5–5%. This is an anecdote, not a confirmed bug — [Shopify Community](https://community.shopify.com/t/calculating-tariffs-and-shipping-to-the-us-from-the-uk/562214)

### Cited Findings: what they pay today
- Customs broker fees run about $100–$250 per entry for full service, or $150–$400 or more for standard commercial entries. Informal entries cost about $75–$150, ISF about $30–$75, and add-ons (classification, post-entry amendments) about $15–$100 each. Some brokers charge 0.5–1.5% of value instead. These are vendor guides — [GingerControl](https://gingercontrol.com/blog/customs-fees-explained); [Suaid](https://suaidglobal.com/insights/customs-broker-fees-2026/); [Strix](https://strixsmart.com/resources/blog/customs-broker-cost)
- MPF for fiscal 2025 is 0.3464%, with a minimum of $33.58 and a maximum of $651.50 — [GingerControl](https://gingercontrol.com/blog/customs-fees-explained)
- Refund recovery: firms work on contingency. McCune Wright models 20% on a $2M claim. Claim traders reportedly pay 40–50 cents on the dollar. Both are self-interested law-firm sources — [McCune Wright](https://mccunewright.com/practice-areas/commercial-litigation/the-ieepa-tariff-ruling-and-your-business); [Ayar Law](https://ayarlaw.com/tariff-refund-attorney); [Leyton](https://leyton.com/us/ieepa-customs-tariff-refund/)
- Enterprise GTM: e2open is quote-only, with third-party estimates of about $200K–$700K per year for a focused deployment and seven figures for broad ones. ONESOURCE (Thomson Reuters) and Descartes do not publish prices — [rfp.wiki](https://www.rfp.wiki/supply-chain-logistics-transportation/transportation-logistics/global-trade-management-software/thomson-reuters-onesource-global-trade/e2open-global-trade-management)

### Inferences
- Pain is no longer a one-time shock. It is a series of announcements, often every few weeks. That favors alerts sold as a subscription over one-off calculator use.
- Refunds are the largest dollar pain. CAPE is self-serve but needs entry-level data and ACH, and it excludes some entries. That creates demand for "which of my entries qualify, and what am I owed?", which is capability (e). The 20% contingency benchmark suggests importers will pay a meaningful share of what is recovered.
- The broker fee (about $100–$400 per entry) is an anchor for small importers. A $19–$49/month tool costs about the same as one entry per month. That is easy to justify if it prevents one misclassification or one surprise bill.

### Gaps
- I found no October 2026 status for CAPE phases beyond May 2026, no Federal Circuit merits decision on Section 122, and no list of Section 232 changes in 2026.
- I found no independent survey of what small importers spend on trade software or consultants.

## 3. Segments: willingness to pay for (a) to (e) and multi-client needs

### Takeaway
The best self-serve fit is small and lower-mid importers (about 10–500 HTS/COO pairs), including former de minimis and Amazon/DTC brands. They pay $20–$150 per month for alerts and impact analysis, and much more (success-fee style) for refunds and audits. Brokers, forwarders, and consultants are multi-client and need client workspaces or white-label. Enterprise buys suites and is a poor self-serve fit. A $9 calculator alone has weak willingness to pay because free calculators are plentiful.

### Cited Findings: competitor and adjacent pricing (all vendor-reported)
- TariffDesk (HTS change alerts): $29/month for 10 codes, $59/month for 50 codes, $99/month for 500 codes — [TariffDesk](https://tariffdesk.com/); [ustariffrates comparison](https://ustariffrates.com/best/best-trade-compliance-software)
- US Tariff Rates: free plan (5 watchlist alerts), Pro $49/month or $399/year, Team $199/month or $1,590/year — [US Tariff Rates pricing](https://ustariffrates.com/pricing)
- TariffClassify: one free watch, paid plans with watchlists up to 50 codes. Prices were not shown — [TariffClassify](https://tariffclassify.ai/tariff-rate-alerts)
- Gaia Dynamics: free with 30 credits a year, paid from $99/month (billed yearly). GingerLite: $150/month for 300 credits — [ustariffrates comparison](https://ustariffrates.com/best/best-trade-compliance-software); [GingerControl](https://gingercontrol.com/blog/hts-software-guide)
- A third-party comparison lists HTS Hero at "$14.99/mo unlimited calculations, Tracker from $49/mo." This may be stale or inaccurate compared with HTS Hero's current pricing — [ustariffrates comparison](https://ustariffrates.com/best/best-trade-compliance-software)
- Free or cheap alternatives: Amazon-seller tariff-price calculators (free), Fiverr HTS classification gigs, Apify duty APIs — [My Amazon Guy](https://myamazonguy.com/us-tariff-price-calculator/); [Fiverr](https://fiverr.com/mojoglobal/classify-your-hts-codes-and-calculate-us-import-duty); [Apify](https://apify.com/tariffsapi/us-import-duty-tariff-calculator)
- Self-filing software is advertised at about $30 per entry, compared with broker fees. This is a vendor claim — [Strix](https://strixsmart.com/pricing)

### Inferences
- Willingness to pay by capability:
  - (a) The calculator alone is a commodity. Free tools are everywhere, and the $9/month evidence is weak. It works best as a free acquisition tool, which matches HTS Hero's current free-calculator decision.
  - (b) Alerts are clearly anchored by competitors at about $29–$99/month for 10–500 codes. Pricing per HTS+COO pair fits the market, because TariffDesk prices per code but ignores origin. Origin-aware alerts are a real differentiator, since stacked measures vary by country.
  - (c) and (d) Impact and savings should sit in higher tiers ($149–$249 or more). Buyers are sourcing and finance teams at mid-market firms who already pay consultants.
  - (e) Audits and refunds have the highest willingness to pay. The benchmarks are 20% contingency and 40–50 cents-on-the-dollar claim sales. A flat fee or a low success fee would undercut lawyers. The licensing risk is that HTS Hero must "find, flag, explain" and leave filing to a broker.
- Buyers vs users:
  - Small firms: the owner or operations lead is both buyer and user.
  - Mid-market: procurement, sourcing, or trade compliance staff are users. The VP Operations, CFO, or controller is the buyer, and the purchase is justified on margin or refund dollars.
  - Brokers and forwarders: the principal or branch manager buys, and the entry writers and account managers use it.
- Purchase triggers:
  - New tariff announcements (Section 301 actions, Section 232 expansions).
  - A surprise duty bill or a broker's invoice.
  - Refund deadlines and CAPE phases (for example, liquidation timing).
  - Sourcing decisions (moving production from China to Vietnam or Mexico).
  - CBP audits, CF-28/29 requests.
  - Annual budgeting and repricing.
- Multi-client segments (brokers, forwarders, consultants) need:
  - client workspaces;
  - bulk import of client entries (ACE ES-003 / ACH reports);
  - white-label or branded alert emails;
  - per-client billing.
  These customers could pay $249–$1,000+ per month but need a sales-assisted motion.
- Highest-ROI ICP for self-serve, pricing by pair count: a small or mid importer with about 20–200 HTS/COO pairs that paid IEEPA duties and sources from China, Vietnam, or other affected origins.

### Gaps
- I found no Reddit threads directly (search returned vendor pages). There is no first-party forum quote on willingness to pay.
- There are no conversion or churn data for tariff-alert SaaS, and no independent survey of willingness to pay for tariff software among SMB importers.
- There is no count of trade compliance consultants or customs attorneys.
- There is no data on how many importers by size actually filed CAPE claims or how much was refunded through October 2026.
