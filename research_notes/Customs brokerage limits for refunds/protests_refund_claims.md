# Who may prepare and file US Customs protests and refund claims for importers (and can an unlicensed, non-attorney software founder do it for pay?)

Research date: 2026-10-16. US law. Primary regulation text was pulled verbatim from the eCFR versioner API (title 19, as of 2026-10-01) and statute text from Cornell LII. Everything else is labeled as secondary.

Bottom line, by activity type (details and citations follow):
- **(a) Advice and analysis** (education, tariff exposure, which duties might be refundable, deadline tracking, ACE report analysis for the importer's own information): generally outside "customs business" as long as it does not cross into preparing documents meant to be filed with CBP. This is the safe zone for an unlicensed founder. The line is fuzzy, and CBP rulings draw it narrowly.
- **(b) Preparing a protest (CBP Form 19 or ACE protest) that the importer signs and files itself:** this is customs business by the statute's own words ("preparation of documents ... intended to be filed with [CBP] ... whether or not signed or filed by the preparer"). The importer's own-account exemption covers only the importer and its regular employees, not an outside preparer. An unlicensed person doing this for another party faces a penalty of up to $10,000 per transaction (19 U.S.C. 1641(b)(6)). **Not allowed without a broker license.**
- **(c) Preparing a protest that a licensed broker or attorney then files:** for a broker, still not allowed. The preparation itself is the regulated act, and CBP held in HQ H326926 that unlicensed data keying is customs business even when a licensed broker transmits the entry. Brokers are also barred from deals where fees "inure to the benefit of" an unlicensed person (19 CFR 111.36(b)) and from lending their license (111.37). For an attorney, the regulations let attorneys file protests (174.3(a)(1)), and a nonlawyer working under a lawyer's supervision is the usual structure. But I found no CBP ruling on whether an outside unlicensed preparer working for a law firm is itself conducting customs business. **This is unresolved, so treat it as risky.**
- **(d) Filing as agent:** the regulations name attorneys and licensed brokers, with built-in authority declarations. A generic "agent" with a power of attorney appears in 19 CFR 174.3/174.12(a)(7), but 19 U.S.C. 1641(b)(1) bars anyone other than a licensed broker from conducting customs business for others. **Not allowed for an unlicensed, non-attorney business acting for paying clients.** CAPE (the IEEPA refund system) is narrower still: only the importer of record or the licensed broker who filed the entries may submit a declaration.

## Q1. Who may file a protest: statute (19 U.S.C. 1514(c)(2)) and regulations (19 CFR 174.3, 174.12, 174.13; part 141 subpart C)

### Takeaway
The statute and 19 CFR 174.12(a) let the importer or consignee (and certain others) file, plus "any authorized agent," subject to the power-of-attorney rule in 174.3. Section 174.3 excuses attorneys at law and licensed customhouse brokers from filing a separate power of attorney and requires one from every other agent. Part 174 itself does not say that only brokers or attorneys may be agents. That limit comes from the broker licensing statute, 19 U.S.C. 1641 (see Q2).

### Cited Findings
- **19 U.S.C. 1514(c)(2)**: subject to sections 1485(d) and 1557(b), protests may be filed by "the importers or consignees shown on the entry papers, or their sureties;" "any person paying any charge or exaction;" "any person seeking entry or delivery;" "any person filing a claim for drawback;" certain USMCA exporters and producers; and "any authorized agent of any of the persons described in clauses (A) through (E)." — [19 U.S.C. 1514, Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/19/1514)
- **19 U.S.C. 1514(c)(1)**: a protest "shall be filed in writing, or transmitted electronically," and "Only one protest may be filed for each entry of merchandise" (with a per-category exception). — [19 U.S.C. 1514](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/19/1514)
- **19 CFR 174.12(a)** (verbatim): "(a) By whom filed. Protests may be filed by: (1) The importer or consignee shown on the entry papers, or their sureties; (2) Any person paying or receiving a refund of any charge or exaction; (3) Any person seeking entry or delivery; (4) Any person filing a claim for drawback; (5) [NAFTA exporter or producer] ...; (6) [USMCA exporter or producer] ...; or (7) Any authorized agent of any of the persons described in paragraphs (a)(1) through (6) of this section, subject to the provisions of § 174.3." — [eCFR 19 CFR part 174](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-174)
- **19 CFR 174.3(a)** (verbatim): "(a) When required. When a protest is filed by a person acting as agent or attorney in fact for the principal, other than an attorney at law or a customhouse broker or his authorized employee acting in his behalf, there shall have been filed or shall be filed with the protest a power of attorney which either specifically authorizes such agent to make, sign, and file the protest or grants unlimited authority to such agent. No power of attorney to file a protest shall be required in the following cases:" — [eCFR 19 CFR 174.3](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-174/subpart-A/section-174.3)
- **19 CFR 174.3(a)(1) Attorney at law** (verbatim): "When the protest is filed by an attorney at law as agent or attorney for the principal, the signing of the protest as agent or attorney for the principal by the attorney at law shall be considered a declaration by him that he is currently a member in good standing of the highest court of a State, possession, territory, commonwealth, or the District of Columbia, and has been authorized to sign and file the protest for the principal." — [eCFR 19 CFR 174.3](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-174/subpart-A/section-174.3)
- **19 CFR 174.3(a)(2) Customhouse broker** (verbatim): "When a protest is filed by a customhouse broker, or an authorized employee acting in his behalf, as agent or attorney in fact for the principal, the signing of the protest by the customhouse broker or an authorized employee in his behalf shall be considered a declaration by the broker that he or the employee signing in his behalf, is authorized to sign and file the protest for the principal. The customhouse broker shall have, however, a general power of attorney to transact Customs business for the principal on Customs Form 5291." — [eCFR 19 CFR 174.3](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-174/subpart-A/section-174.3)
- **174.3(b) to (d)**: a corporate power of attorney "shall be signed by a duly authorized officer or employee of the corporation." Partnership powers of attorney are limited to 2 years, all others "may be granted for an unlimited period," and any power of attorney may be revoked "by written notice given to and received by CBP." — [eCFR 19 CFR 174.3](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-174/subpart-A/section-174.3)
- **19 CFR 174.12(c) Identity of filer** (verbatim): "The identity of the person filing the protest or his agent, or attorney shall be noted on the protest. This may be accomplished through a signature which is handwritten in ink, stamped, typed, facsimile, telefax, or by electronic certification in CBP Automated Commercial Environment (ACE) or any other CBP-authorized electronic data interchange system. If the person filing the protest is not the importer of record or consignee, the filer shall include his address and importer number, if any." — [eCFR part 174](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-174)
- **19 CFR 174.12(b)**: a written protest is filed "in quadruplicate on CBP Form 19 or a form of the same size clearly labeled 'Protest'," and may also be "transmitted electronically pursuant to any electronic data interchange system authorized by CBP." — [eCFR part 174](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-174)
- **19 CFR 174.13(a)(1) to (2)** (verbatim): a protest shall contain "(1) The name and address of the protestant, i.e., the importer of record or consignee, and the name and address of his agent or attorney if signed by one of these; (2) The importer number of the protestant. If the protestant is represented by an agent having power of attorney, the importer number of the agent shall also be shown;". Section (a)(6) requires "The nature of, and justification for the objection set forth distinctly and specifically with respect to each category, payment, claim, decision, or refusal." Section (a)(9) requires a declaration on whether the entry is or could be subject to drawback. — [eCFR part 174](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-174)
- **19 CFR 174.13(c) refund designee** (verbatim language that may be appended): "any refunds with respect to the entry under protest shall be issued electronically in accordance with 31 U.S.C. 3332, unless a waiver condition in 31 CFR 208.4 is met, to the agent designated by the importer/consignee:___". The regulation adds: "This designation supersedes any existing designation previously authorized on CBP Form 4811." — [eCFR part 174](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-174)
- **19 CFR 174.14(d)**: only the original filer, "or its agent subject to § 174.3," may amend a protest. — [eCFR part 174](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-174)
- **19 CFR 141.31(a)** (verbatim): "A power of attorney may be executed for the transaction by an agent or attorney of a specified part or all the Customs business of the principal." 141.31(c): "A power of attorney to a minor shall not be accepted." — [eCFR 19 CFR part 141](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-141)
- **19 CFR 141.32**: "Customs Form 5291 may be used for giving power of attorney to transact Customs business"; otherwise it must be a general power of attorney with unlimited authority, or a limited one "as explicit in its terms and executed in the same manner as a Customs Form 5291." — [eCFR part 141](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-141)
- **19 CFR 141.46** (verbatim): "Before transacting Customs business in the name of his principal, a customhouse broker is required to obtain a valid power of attorney to do so. He is not required to file the power of attorney with CBP." — [eCFR part 141](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-141)
- **19 CFR 111.40** (verbatim): "A broker must not act on behalf of any person, or attempt to represent any person, regarding any protest unless he is authorized to do so in accordance with part 174 of this chapter." — [eCFR 19 CFR part 111](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-111)

### Inferences
- Read alone, part 174 contemplates three kinds of agent filer: an attorney (no power of attorney filed), a licensed broker (power of attorney held on CF 5291, not filed), and "other" agents who must file a specific or unlimited power of attorney with the protest. That third category is what prior AI answers probably misread as permission for anyone holding a power of attorney. It is not, because 19 U.S.C. 1641(b)(1) independently bars conducting customs business for others without a license (Q2). The third category most plausibly covers non-commercial or related-party agents: an officer, a related company within the corporate-compliance carve-out, or an unpaid individual for a non-commercial shipment under 141.33 and 111.2(a)(2)(v). It does not cover a for-hire service business.
- "Customhouse broker" in part 174 is the old term for a licensed customs broker.

### Gaps
- I found no CBP ruling or court case that applies 174.3's "other agent with power of attorney" path to an unlicensed commercial consultant. I could not confirm how CBP would treat a protest filed by such an agent (accepted, denied, or treated as void). See Q2 gaps.

## Q2. Is preparing a protest "customs business" (19 U.S.C. 1641; 19 CFR 111.1, 111.2, 111.4)? Rulings and cases on unlicensed persons

### Takeaway
Yes. The statutory definition of "customs business" expressly covers transactions with CBP about classification, payment of duties, and "the refund, rebate, or drawback thereof," plus "the preparation of documents ... intended to be filed with [CBP] ... whether or not signed or filed by the preparer." A protest seeking a refund for someone else is squarely inside that definition, and doing it for another party requires a broker license. The only carve-outs are acting for one's own account (the importer and its regular employees), mere electronic transmission of data received for transmission, and "corporate compliance activity" between related entities over 50% common ownership. None of these fits an outside software or consulting firm.

### Cited Findings
- **19 U.S.C. 1641(a)(2)** (verbatim): "The term 'customs business' means those activities involving transactions with U.S. Customs and Border Protection concerning the entry and admissibility of merchandise, its classification and valuation, the payment of duties, taxes, or other charges assessed or collected by U.S. Customs and Border Protection upon merchandise by reason of its importation, or the refund, rebate, or drawback thereof. It also includes the preparation of documents or forms in any format and the electronic transmission of documents, invoices, bills, or parts thereof, intended to be filed with U.S. Customs and Border Protection in furtherance of such activities, whether or not signed or filed by the preparer, or activities relating to such preparation, but does not include the mere electronic transmission of data received for transmission to Customs." — [19 U.S.C. 1641, Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/19/1641)
- **19 U.S.C. 1641(b)(1)** (verbatim): "No person may conduct customs business (other than solely on behalf of that person) unless that person holds a valid customs broker's license issued by the Secretary under paragraph (2) or (3)." — [19 U.S.C. 1641](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/19/1641)
- **19 U.S.C. 1641(b)(6)** (verbatim, in part): "Any person who intentionally transacts customs business, other than solely on the behalf of that person, without holding a valid customs broker's license granted to that person under this subsection shall be liable to the United States for a monetary penalty not to exceed $10,000 for each such transaction as well as for each violation of any other provision of this section." — [19 U.S.C. 1641](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/19/1641)
- **19 U.S.C. 1641(b)(2) to (3)**: only a US-citizen individual may hold an individual license. A corporation, association, or partnership may be licensed if at least one officer or member holds an individual license. — [19 U.S.C. 1641](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/19/1641)
- **19 CFR 111.1 "Customs business"** (verbatim): "'Customs business' means those activities involving transactions with CBP concerning the entry and admissibility of merchandise, its classification and valuation, the payment of duties, taxes, or other charges assessed or collected by CBP on merchandise by reason of its importation, and the refund, rebate, or drawback of those duties, taxes, or other charges. 'Customs business' also includes the preparation, and activities relating to the preparation, of documents in any format and the electronic transmission of documents and parts of documents intended to be filed with CBP in furtherance of any other customs business activity, whether or not signed or filed by the preparer. However, 'customs business' does not include the mere electronic transmission of data received for transmission to CBP and does not include a corporate compliance activity." — [eCFR 19 CFR 111.1](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-111)
- **19 CFR 111.1 "Corporate compliance activity"** (verbatim, in part): "activity performed by a business entity to ensure that documents for a related business entity or entities are prepared and filed with CBP using 'reasonable care', but such activity does not extend to the actual preparation or filing of the documents or their electronic equivalents." "Related" means more than 50% ownership links. — [eCFR 19 CFR 111.1](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-111)
- **19 CFR 111.2(a)(2)(i)** (verbatim): "An importer or exporter transacting customs business solely on his own account and in no sense on behalf of another is not required to be licensed, nor are his authorized regular employees or officers who act only for him in the transaction of such business." — [eCFR 19 CFR part 111](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-111)
- **19 CFR 111.4** (verbatim): "Any person who intentionally transacts customs business, other than as provided in § 111.2(a)(2), without holding a valid broker's license, will be liable for a monetary penalty for each such transaction as well as for each violation of any other provision of 19 U.S.C. 1641." — [eCFR part 111](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-111)
- **19 CFR 111.36(b)** (verbatim): "a broker must not enter into any agreement with an unlicensed person to transact customs business for others in such manner that the fees or other benefits resulting from the services rendered for others inure to the benefit of the unlicensed person." The only exception, in (c), is referral compensation to freight forwarders. — [eCFR part 111](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-111)
- **19 CFR 111.37** (verbatim): "A broker must not allow his license, permit or name to be used by or for any unlicensed person ..., other than his own employees authorized to act for him, in the solicitation, promotion or performance of any customs business or transaction." — [eCFR part 111](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-111)
- **CBP HQ H326926 (Dec. 19, 2023)**, reported secondarily: an offshore, unlicensed company that extracted and keyed entry data for a licensed broker was conducting impermissible customs business, even though the broker transmitted. CBP also said an unlicensed company should not have ABI access. — [International Trade Insights (secondary)](https://www.internationaltradeinsights.com/2023/12/cbp-further-defines-customs-business-in-new-ruling/); [Tradewin blog (secondary)](https://blog.tradewin.net/cbp-clarifies-definition-of-transacting-customs-business)
- **CBP HQ H350722 (Jan. 16, 2026)**, reported secondarily, on an unlicensed online import platform. CBP said an unlicensed entity may not act as intermediary between broker and importer if it is "actively participating in decisions and activities relating to the preparation or filing of Customs documents." It also "cannot decide what data should appear on an entry," whether extraction is manual or OCR. Classification beyond six-digit HTS is customs business, so "only derives potential HTSUS subheadings to the six-digit level" is permissible. And "only a licensed customs broker may complete and submit CBP Form 5106 on behalf of another party." The platform was found compliant because it did not decide what went to the broker and did not transmit to CBP. — [Customs & International Trade Law blog, Mar. 30, 2026 (secondary)](https://customsandinternationaltradelaw.com/2026/03/30/even-ai-needs-a-license-know-when-automation-unlawfully-crosses-into-customs-business/); [Mondaq repost](https://www.mondaq.com/unitedstates/international-trade-investment/1766374/even-ai-needs-a-license-know-when-automation-unlawfully-crosses-into-customs-business); [Woodland Group (secondary)](https://www.woodlandgroup.com/news/cbp-clarifies-limits-on-unlicensed-customs-services)
- A vendor guide says unlicensed consultants, software vendors, and refund platforms cannot file CF-19 protests, CAPE claims, or drawback claims for a paying client, but that "preparation" for the importer's or broker's use is permitted. It also claims attorneys cannot file protests unless licensed. Both claims conflict with primary text: 1641(a)(2) covers preparation "whether or not signed or filed by the preparer," and 19 CFR 174.3(a)(1) expressly provides for attorney-filed protests. The page also cites a "CSMS 62074401" that I could not verify, and its reviewer is listed as "pending name." **Treat it as unreliable.** — [tariffrefundcredits.com guide (secondary, unreliable)](https://tariffrefundcredits.com/guides/broker-vs-consultant)
- A former US Customs attorney is quoted describing a protest as an administrative filing that "a customs broker or even a company can handle internally." That is consistent with the own-account exemption, not with third-party preparation. — [CE Pro, "Tariff Refunds: Crucial Advice from a Former U.S. Customs Attorney" (secondary)](https://www.cepro.com/news/tariff-refunds-crucial-advice-from-a-former-u-s-customs-attorney/625994/)

### Inferences
- **(a) Advice and analysis.** Not customs business if it stays general or analytical: explaining which duties may be refundable, estimating exposure, flagging liquidation dates and protest deadlines, educational content, and analysis the importer's own staff then use to decide and prepare. Risk rises as the deliverable approaches "the document to be filed." H350722's test asks whether the unlicensed party "decides what data should appear" on a filing. A software tool that auto-generates the entry list, HTS claims, and the protest's "nature of and justification for the objection" text for a specific importer looks like preparation of a document intended to be filed. CBP's six-digit-HTS line suggests it reads "customs business" broadly where automation is involved.
- **(b) Preparing a protest the importer files itself.** The statute's "whether or not signed or filed by the preparer" language removes the "the importer signed it" defense. The own-account exemption (111.2(a)(2)(i)) covers the importer's "regular employees or officers," not contractors. So drafting a protest for pay, even a fill-in-the-blanks CF-19 the client signs, is customs business. **Not permitted without a license.**
- **(c) Preparing for a broker to file.** Same problem, made worse: H326926 shows CBP treats an unlicensed preparer as conducting customs business even when the broker files. A broker who shares refund-based fees with the unlicensed preparer risks violating 111.36(b) and 111.37. A compliant structure is the reverse: the broker or a broker-owned entity does the preparation, and the software company sells the broker or importer a tool, or is paid by the importer for advice only, not per filing.
- **(d) Filing as agent.** Blocked by 1641(b)(1) unless the founder is licensed or is an attorney.
- **Attorneys.** 1641 contains no express attorney exemption, but 19 CFR 174.3(a)(1) treats attorney-filed protests as routine, and law firms file protests and CIT cases in practice. Whether an attorney "conducts customs business" when preparing protests is not settled in anything I found, so treat it as an open question (see Gaps).
- **Fastest compliant path for the founder:** (1) pass the Customs Broker License Exam and get licensed, possibly as a corporate license with the founder as licensed officer, which makes protest preparation and filing lawful; (2) partner so that a licensed broker or attorney does all preparation and filing under its own engagement and billing, while HTS Hero supplies analysis or a tool the professional controls; or (3) sell self-serve software to importers, who prepare and file on their own account. Option (3) still risks looking like "preparation" if the software, rather than the importer, decides the filing's content.

### Gaps
- I could not reach the full text of H326926 or H350722 on rulings.cbp.gov (summaries are secondary). Pull both from CROSS to confirm exact language.
- I found no CBP ruling or court decision voiding or denying a protest because an unauthorized or unlicensed agent filed it. I could not confirm whether CBP rejects such protests or accepts them and penalizes the filer.
- I found no CBP ruling on whether an attorney (or an unlicensed consultant working under an attorney) preparing protests is "customs business" requiring a license.
- I did not verify the current inflation-adjusted penalty amount (the statute says $10,000; the vendor page claims $5,000 to $30,000).

## Q3. Timing: 180-day protest window, 19 U.S.C. 1520(d) post-importation FTA claims, repeal of 1520(c), reliquidation

### Takeaway
A protest must be filed within 180 days after liquidation or reliquidation, or after the decision being protested (90 days for entries made before Dec. 18, 2004). 1520(c) (clerical error and mistake of fact) was repealed for entries on or after Dec. 18, 2004. 1520(d) allows a post-importation FTA preference claim within 1 year of importation, even without a protest.

### Cited Findings
- **19 U.S.C. 1514(c)(3)**: a protest "shall be filed with the Customs Service within 180 days after but not before" (A) the date of liquidation or reliquidation, or (B) where (A) does not apply, the date of the decision protested. Sureties get 180 days from the date of mailing of a demand for payment. — [19 U.S.C. 1514](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/19/1514)
- **19 CFR 174.12(e)**: the deadline is "within 90 days of a decision relating to an entry made before December 18, 2004, or within 180 days of a decision relating to an entry made on or after December 18, 2004." It runs from the notice or date of liquidation or reliquidation (per §§ 159.9 or 159.10), the date of a non-liquidation decision (including "written notice of a denial of a claim filed under section 520(d)"), or a surety's demand. 174.12(f): the filing date is the date CBP receives the protest. — [eCFR part 174](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-174)
- **19 U.S.C. 1520(c)** was repealed by Pub. L. 108-429, title II, § 2105 (Dec. 3, 2004), effective for merchandise entered or withdrawn on or after the 15th day after Dec. 3, 2004. — [19 U.S.C. 1520, Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/19/1520)
- **19 U.S.C. 1520(d)**: "Notwithstanding the fact that a valid protest was not filed," CBP may reliquidate to refund excess duties (including MPF) for goods qualifying under FTA rules of origin where no preference claim was made at entry. The claim must be filed within 1 year after importation, with a written declaration, copies of certificates or certifications of origin, and other documents CBP requires. — [19 U.S.C. 1520](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/19/1520)
- **19 U.S.C. 1514(a)** lists the protestable decisions: appraised value, classification and rate and amount of duties, "all charges or exactions of whatever character within the jurisdiction of the Secretary of the Treasury," exclusion, liquidation or reliquidation, refusal to pay drawback, and refusal to reliquidate under 1520(d). Such decisions are final unless protested. — [19 U.S.C. 1514](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/19/1514)
- **28 U.S.C. 2631(a)**: a CIT action contesting a protest denial "may be commenced in the Court of International Trade by the person who filed the protest" (or a surety). Under 2631(i), residual actions may be brought "by any person adversely affected or aggrieved by agency action." — [28 U.S.C. 2631, Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/28/2631)
- A 28 U.S.C. 1581(i) residual-jurisdiction suit generally must be filed within 2 years after the cause of action accrues (28 U.S.C. 2636). The trigger date for IEEPA claims is contested. — [Ayar Law guide (secondary)](https://ayarlaw.com/tariff-refund-resources/cbp-protest-tariff-refund); [Ward and Smith (secondary)](https://www.wardandsmith.com/article/after-learning-resources-liquidation-protests-and-ieepa-tariff-refunds)

### Inferences
- Liquidation usually occurs about 314 days after entry. That fact comes from general knowledge, not a source fetched here. Practically, the protest window for an entry closes about 16 months after entry.
- A protest's validity (timely, specific, filed by an authorized party) controls CIT jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. 1581(a), so a defectively filed protest can cost the client its refund rights entirely. This is a strong practical reason not to have an unauthorized party sign or file.

## Q4. Section 301 exclusion refunds, IEEPA refunds (CAPE), and Section 122 refunds for liquidated entries

### Takeaway
- **Section 301 exclusions:** CBP guidance says a Post Summary Correction is the route for unliquidated entries and a protest within 180 days of liquidation is the route for liquidated ones. DOJ has argued, successfully, that liquidated entries without a timely protest cannot get exclusion refunds.
- **IEEPA:** CBP's CAPE portal handles refunds. Only the importer of record or the licensed broker who filed the entries may submit a declaration. Entries under open protest and finally liquidated entries were excluded from early phases. Phase 3 (announced for Oct. 6, 2026) reportedly covers finally liquidated entries only for CIT plaintiffs with reliquidation orders. The government's position is that older entries need a 1581(i) suit, not a protest.
- **Section 122:** there is no CBP refund portal, the appeal is pending, and protestability is disputed.

### Cited Findings
- **CBP IEEPA refunds page, CAPE filer eligibility** (CBP's wording as relayed by the fetch tool): "Only the IOR or the licensed customs broker who filed the entries can file a CAPE Declaration." The filer must be associated with the entries through its ACE filer code prefix. Refunds go to the importer of record or a party it designates on CBP Form 4811 or in its ACE Portal account, and bank information in an ACE Portal account is required. — [CBP, IEEPA Duty Refunds](https://www.cbp.gov/trade/programs-administration/trade-remedies/ieepa-duty-refunds)
- The same CBP page says entries on an open or suspended protest are currently rejected ("Entry cannot be on an Open or Suspended protest"), and entries liquidated more than 80 days earlier are rejected. Both categories are listed for "subsequent deployments." CSMS messages cited: #68340863 (Apr. 20, 2026 deployment), #68569567, #69035485, #69127837. — [CBP, IEEPA Duty Refunds](https://www.cbp.gov/trade/programs-administration/trade-remedies/ieepa-duty-refunds)
- Law-firm and vendor summaries agree that lawyers and other third parties cannot file CAPE declarations directly, and that filing happens via the ACE Portal, not ABI. — [Holland & Knight, Apr. 2026 (secondary)](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/04/cape-has-arrived-navigating-the-next-phase-of-ieepa-duty-refunds); [GHY (secondary)](https://www.ghy.com/?p=26178)
- The CIT (Judge Richard K. Eaton) issued orders on July 15 and July 21, 2026 directing reliquidation of certain finally liquidated entries without IEEPA duties for refund plaintiffs. These orders affect about 3,700 related suits. CBP reported about $86.3 billion in refunds processed as of July 15. The government appealed to the Federal Circuit, arguing that the CIT lacked authority to order refunds for non-plaintiffs, and the appeal is pending. — [Morgan Lewis, July 2026 (secondary)](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2026/07/reliquidation-and-relief-trade-court-orders-ieepa-refunds-for-finally-liquidated-entries)
- **CAPE Phase 3**: per a Sept. 18, 2026 law-firm article, "CBP will deploy Phase 3 on October 6." It covers finally liquidated entries for plaintiffs with a CIT reliquidation order who gave CBP a valid importer-of-record number by July 30, 2026. Importers without active CIT litigation are not eligible. — [Coppersmith, Sept. 18, 2026 (secondary)](https://www.coppersmith.com/cape-phase-3-ieepa-refunds-october-2026/); [Strix (secondary)](https://strixsmart.com/resources/blog/cape-phase-3-liquidated-entries)
- The government's position, per a Sept. 18, 2026 law-firm article, is that the established route for older entries is a 1581(i) action, then a court order directing reliquidation, then CAPE Phase 3. Current authority does not establish that an IEEPA-only protest is either required or sufficient. An importer that filed an IEEPA-only protest may withdraw it, if the entry is otherwise eligible and within 80 days of liquidation, and use CAPE. — [Ward and Smith, Sept. 18, 2026 (secondary)](https://www.wardandsmith.com/article/after-learning-resources-liquidation-protests-and-ieepa-tariff-refunds)
- There is commentary that CBP may treat IEEPA collections as non-protestable "ministerial" acts, and the government has stipulated it will not oppose reliquidation only for plaintiffs with 1581(i) actions. — [Norton Rose Fulbright (secondary)](https://www.nortonrosefulbright.com/en/knowledge/publications/585c30e7/ieepa-tariff-refund-rights-key-considerations-before-the-supreme-court-ruling); [Hunton (secondary)](https://www.hunton.com/insights/legal/file-or-die-if-the-supreme-court-strikes-down-ieepa-tariffs-must-importers-sue-for-refunds)
- **Section 301 exclusions**: CBP's May 2020 guidance (CSMS #42566154, as reported) says an importer of record may request a refund via a PSC for unliquidated entries at least 15 days before scheduled liquidation, or "by filing a protest for entries that have liquidated but where the protest period (180 days from the date of liquidation) has not expired." — [ArentFox Schiff (secondary)](https://www.afslaw.com/perspectives/alerts/customs-issues-guidance-how-preserve-your-rights-refunds-china-section-301); [International Trade Insights, May 2020 (secondary)](https://www.internationaltradeinsights.com/2020/05/cbp-issues-guidance-update-on-section-232-and-section-301-exclusion-refunds/)

- The Federal Circuit in *ARP Materials* held that CBP's assessments on entries later covered by retroactive Section 301 exclusions were protestable under § 1514. — [Ward and Smith (secondary)](https://www.wardandsmith.com/article/after-learning-resources-liquidation-protests-and-ieepa-tariff-refunds)
- **Section 122**: the CIT ruled 2-1 on May 7, 2026 that the 10% surcharge exceeded presidential authority, with relief limited to three plaintiffs. The surcharge ran from Feb. 24 to July 24, 2026. The Federal Circuit stayed the judgment, and as of Sept. 2026 no merits ruling had issued. — [Skadden, May 2026 (secondary)](https://www.skadden.com/insights/publications/2026/05/us-trade-court-strikes-down-section-122-tariffs); [Strix, Sept. 8, 2026 (secondary)](https://strixsmart.com/resources/blog/section-122-refund-status); [ABA Procurement Lawyer, Fall 2026 (secondary)](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/public_contract_law/resources/procurement-lawyer/2026-fall/ieepa-tariffs-struck-down-replacement-tariffs-expired/)
- There is no CBP refund portal for Section 122 duties. Most firms recommend protective protests within 180 days of liquidation, while "at least one major firm argues these presidentially-imposed duties may not be protestable at all." — [Tariff Refund Solutions (secondary, vendor)](https://tariffrefundsolutions.com/section-122-tariff-refund); [Borderless (secondary)](https://borderless.us/blog/section-122-surcharge-refunds-2026)

### Inferences
- For a software founder, every refund route (PSC, protest, CAPE, CIT suit) runs through one of three parties: the importer itself, the importer's licensed broker, or an attorney (CIT only). None runs through an unlicensed third party. CAPE is the narrowest: it is not even open to attorneys, nor to a broker other than the one that filed the entries.
- Because CAPE refunds can go to a Form 4811 designee, a refund firm could be named as payee. This raises the Anti-Assignment Act question in Q6, and if the firm prepared the filing, the designation does not cure the licensing problem.

## Q5. Unauthorized practice of law: non-lawyers in protest proceedings and before the Court of International Trade

### Takeaway
At the CIT, corporations must appear through an attorney admitted to that court, and only an individual may appear pro se (CIT Rule 75(b)(1)). Non-lawyer, non-broker representation of a corporate importer in court is not possible. At CBP, brokers may represent clients before DHS on matters concerning merchandise they handle (19 CFR 111.5(a)), and attorneys may file protests (174.3(a)(1)). A non-lawyer giving legal advice for pay about refund rights raises state UPL questions that I could not resolve with primary sources.

### Cited Findings
- **CIT Rule 75(b)(1)**, as quoted in CIT opinions: "Except for an individual (not a corporation, partnership, organization[,] or other legal entity) appearing pro se, each party ... must appear through an attorney authorized to practice before the court." — [CIT Slip Op. 22-80, Guangdong Hongteo Technology Co. v. United States](https://www.cit.uscourts.gov/sites/cit/files/22-80.pdf); [CIT Rules and Forms (complete PDF)](https://www.cit.uscourts.gov/sites/cit/files/COMPLETE%20RULES%20AND%20FORMS.pdf)
- "The rule is well established that a corporation must always appear through counsel." (*Lady Kelly, Inc. v. U.S. Sec'y of Agric.*, CIT 2006, as quoted in search results.) — [CIT opinion archive](https://www.cit.uscourts.gov/sites/cit/files/05-151.pdf)
- **19 CFR 111.5(a)**: "A broker who represents a client in the importation or exportation of merchandise may represent the client before the Department of Homeland Security ... on any matter concerning that merchandise." — [eCFR part 111](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-111)
- **19 CFR 111.35**: a broker must not accept from an attorney (directly or via a client arrangement) "on account of any case litigated in any court of law or on account of any other legal service rendered by an attorney any fee or remuneration in excess of an amount measured by or commensurate with the time, effort and skill expended by the broker." — [eCFR part 111](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-111)
- Market practice: broker-attorney partnerships, in which brokers identify entries, compute amounts, and track deadlines while attorneys handle jurisdiction and court filings, are described as increasingly common. — [usacustomsclearance.com (secondary, broker marketing)](https://usacustomsclearance.com/process/ieepa-tariff-refunds/)

### Inferences
- A protest is an administrative filing, so the CIT's attorney rule does not apply at the protest stage. Who may represent at that stage is governed by 1641 and part 174 (Q1 and Q2), not by state UPL law. Any CIT step (a 1581(a) challenge to a protest denial, or a 1581(i) IEEPA or Section 122 suit) requires a CIT-admitted attorney for a corporate importer.
- 111.35 signals CBP's hostility to brokers taking a contingency-style share of litigation outcomes, but it restricts fees from attorneys and does not ban contingency fees generally.
- State UPL risk: a contingency refund firm that tells a specific importer its legal rights (for example, "file suit under 1581(i) or lose your refund") may be giving legal advice. Model Rule 5.4 also generally bars lawyers from sharing legal fees with non-lawyers, which limits revenue-share deals between a software company and a law firm. **I did not fetch a source for this, so verify it.**

### Gaps
- I did not fetch the current text of CIT Rule 75 itself (only quotes in opinions), or CIT Rule 74 on attorney admission.
- I found no state UPL opinion or bar ethics opinion specific to customs or tariff refund firms.
- I found no source on Model Rule 5.4 fee-sharing in this context (inference only).

## Q6. Contingency fees and the Anti-Assignment Act (31 U.S.C. 3727)

### Takeaway
I found no CBP regulation that bans contingency fees outright. The relevant broker-conduct limits are 111.36(b) (no fees inuring to unlicensed persons) and 111.35 (no outsized remuneration from attorneys). The Anti-Assignment Act broadly covers transfers of, interests in, and "authorization to receive payment for" claims against the US before allowance, which is a real risk for refund-purchase or refund-assignment models. Whether CBP's payee-designation regulations (Form 4811, 174.13(c)) amount to a waiver is unresolved.

### Cited Findings
- 31 U.S.C. 3727 defines "assignment" broadly: "a transfer or assignment of any part of a claim against the United States Government or of an interest in the claim," and "the authorization to receive payment for any part of the claim." Questions have been raised about its application to tariff refund investors, and the answer turns on precedent, who may enforce, and whether regulations waive the Act. — [Orrick, Mar. 2026 (secondary)](https://www.orrick.com/en/insights/2026/03/tariff-refund-claims-and-the-anti-assignment-of-claims-act)
- A valid assignment under the Act requires that the claim be allowed, the amount decided, and a warrant issued, with formalities (two witnesses, acknowledgment). — [In re R & L Refunds, Inc. (vLex)](https://case-law.vlex.com/vid/892377958)
- An IRS Chief Counsel memo says the Act's prohibition can be waived by the government and concluded that a pre-allowance purchaser of a tax refund did not become its owner. — [IRS written determination 1111005](https://www.eitc.irs.gov/pub/irs-wd/1111005.pdf)
- 19 CFR 174.13(c) lets the importer designate an agent to receive refunds on a protested entry electronically, and that designation "supersedes any existing designation previously authorized on CBP Form 4811." — [eCFR part 174](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-174)
- 19 CFR 111.36(b) and 111.35: see Q2 and Q5 for verbatim text. — [eCFR part 111](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-111)
- Vendors advertise contingency models: Tariff Refund Solutions offers review on a "Contingency basis, no upfront fees" and calls itself "Attorney-led tariff refund recovery." — [Tariff Refund Solutions (vendor)](https://tariffrefundsolutions.com/section-122-tariff-refund)

### Inferences
- **Safest fee structure:** the importer pays the founder directly (flat fee, subscription, or a percentage of refunds received, paid by invoice after the importer receives its refund). CBP pays the importer, there is no assignment, and the founder is not designated as payee. A percentage-of-refund fee does not by itself cure the licensing issue if the founder prepared the filing.
- Having the founder named as Form 4811 or 174.13(c) refund designee is an "authorization to receive payment" that could implicate the Act, unless CBP's regulations are treated as a waiver. **This is unresolved, so avoid it.**
- If a licensed broker runs a contingency program and pays the founder a share, 111.36(b) is violated if the founder is unlicensed and the fees come from "services rendered for others."

### Gaps
- I found no CBP ruling or court decision applying 31 U.S.C. 3727 to Form 4811 designations or to IEEPA refunds.
- I found no federal rule on attorney contingency fees in CIT refund cases. State ethics rules govern these, and I did not research them.
- I did not fetch the 31 U.S.C. 3727 statute text directly.

## Q7. How third-party refund and audit firms describe who actually files (market structure)

### Takeaway
The firms I could check route actual filing through a licensed broker, the importer itself, or attorneys. Unlicensed firms present themselves as doing "review," "analysis," or "data pulls" on contingency. I could not verify the structures of Tarifflo or One Eagle Advisory.

### Cited Findings
- Tariff Refund Solutions: "Attorney-led tariff refund recovery," contingency review with no upfront fees. It pulls ACE entry data and reviews options "with an attorney," and directs importers to "Speak with an attorney" for CIT filings. It disclaims legal advice and attorney-client relationships. No percentage is disclosed. — [Tariff Refund Solutions](https://tariffrefundsolutions.com/section-122-tariff-refund)
- tariffrefundcredits.com describes a model with a flat platform fee to the consultant plus a per-claim filing fee to a licensed broker, who files "using its own PKI credential and license number" under a power of attorney. It also claims attorneys cannot file protests unless licensed, which contradicts 19 CFR 174.3(a)(1). — [tariffrefundcredits.com (secondary, partly unreliable)](https://tariffrefundcredits.com/guides/broker-vs-consultant)
- Brokers market "preparing and filing protests on the importer's behalf," tracking liquidation dates, and coordinating with legal counsel. — [usacustomsclearance.com](https://usacustomsclearance.com/process/ieepa-tariff-refunds/)
- CBP's own CAPE rules force the structure: the importer of record or its filing broker submits, and refunds go to the importer of record or its Form 4811 designee. — [CBP, IEEPA Duty Refunds](https://www.cbp.gov/trade/programs-administration/trade-remedies/ieepa-duty-refunds)
- Drawback: under 19 U.S.C. 1514(c)(2)(D) and 19 CFR 174.12(a)(4), "any person filing a claim for drawback" may protest. — [19 U.S.C. 1514](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/19/1514)

### Inferences
- The common legitimate structure is **lead generation, analysis, or software on the front end, with a licensed broker or law firm doing the preparation and filing under its own engagement with the importer, and billing the importer itself.** Revenue-sharing back to the unlicensed front end is where 111.36(b) and attorney fee-sharing rules bite. Several market participants likely operate in grey areas.
- Drawback specialists are typically licensed brokers, because drawback claims are "customs business" ("refund, rebate, or drawback").
