# Can an unlicensed person prepare or file drawback, PSCs and CAPE IEEPA refund declarations for importers, for pay?

Scope: US law as of 2026-10-16. Researcher could not reach eCFR or federalregister.gov directly (both redirected to an "unblock" page). Regulation text below comes from Cornell LII's CFR mirror (law.cornell.edu), which reproduces current eCFR text. Quotes are exact but sometimes cut into segments by the fetch tool. HQ H350722's PDF could not be text-extracted locally, so its content comes from law-firm summaries (secondary, labeled).

Four activities used throughout:
- (a) general advice/analysis
- (b) preparing documents that the importer itself files
- (c) preparing documents that a licensed broker reviews and files
- (d) filing on the importer's behalf

## 1. Statutory and regulatory definition of "customs business" and the license exceptions

### Takeaway
The statute and regulation both put "refund, rebate, or drawback" inside "customs business." They also cover *preparing* documents "intended to be filed" with CBP "whether or not signed or filed by the preparer." The only carve-outs are "mere electronic transmission of data received for transmission" and "corporate compliance activity" (related companies at more than 50% common ownership, and even then not actual preparation). The 111.2(a)(2) exceptions cover a person acting on its own account (and its own employees), a broker's own employees, and narrow marine, in-bond, noncommercial and FTZ cases. There is no exception for consultants, drawback specialists, refund firms or software vendors.

### Cited Findings
- 19 U.S.C. 1641(a)(1): "The term 'customs broker' means any person granted a customs broker's license by the Secretary under subsection (b)." — [19 U.S.C. 1641, Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/19/1641)
- 19 U.S.C. 1641(a)(2), verbatim: "The term 'customs business' means those activities involving transactions with U.S. Customs and Border Protection concerning the entry and admissibility of merchandise, its classification and valuation, the payment of duties, taxes, or other charges assessed or collected by U.S. Customs and Border Protection upon merchandise by reason of its importation, or the refund, rebate, or drawback thereof. It also includes the preparation of documents or forms in any format and the electronic transmission of documents, invoices, bills, or parts thereof, intended to be filed with U.S. Customs and Border Protection in furtherance of such activities, whether or not signed or filed by the preparer, or activities relating to such preparation, but does not include the mere electronic transmission of data received for transmission to Customs." — [19 U.S.C. 1641, Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/19/1641)
- 19 U.S.C. 1641(b)(1): "No person may conduct customs business (other than solely on behalf of that person) unless that person holds a valid customs broker's license issued by the Secretary under paragraph (2) or (3)." — [19 U.S.C. 1641, Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/19/1641)
- 19 U.S.C. 1641(b)(2): "The Secretary may grant an individual a customs broker's license only if that individual is a citizen of the United States." — [19 U.S.C. 1641, Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/19/1641)
- 19 CFR 111.1 "Customs business" (current, after the 2022 modernization), verbatim: "'Customs business' means those activities involving transactions with CBP concerning the entry and admissibility of merchandise, its classification and valuation, the payment of duties, taxes, or other charges assessed or collected by CBP on merchandise by reason of its importation, and the refund, rebate, or drawback of those duties, taxes, or other charges. 'Customs business' also includes the preparation, and activities relating to the preparation, of documents in any format and the electronic transmission of documents and parts of documents intended to be filed with CBP in furtherance of any other customs business activity, whether or not signed or filed by the preparer. However, 'customs business' does not include the mere electronic transmission of data received for transmission to CBP and does not include a 'corporate compliance activity'." — [19 CFR 111.1, Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/19/111.1)
- 19 CFR 111.1 "Corporate compliance activity": "activity performed by a business entity to ensure that documents for a related business entity or entities are prepared and filed with CBP using 'reasonable care', but such activity does not extend to the actual preparation or filing of the documents or their electronic equivalents." "Related" means more than 50 percent ownership (parent, subsidiary, or sister entities under common more-than-50% ownership). — [19 CFR 111.1, Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/19/111.1)
- 19 CFR 111.1 "Employee": "a person who meets the common law definition of employee and is in the service of a customs broker." — [19 CFR 111.1, Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/19/111.1)
- 19 CFR 111.1 "Responsible supervision and control": "that degree of supervision and control necessary to ensure the proper transaction of the customs business of a broker, including actions necessary to ensure that an employee of a broker provides substantially the same quality of service..." — [19 CFR 111.1, Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/19/111.1)
- 19 CFR 111.2(a)(1): "a person must obtain the license provided for in this part in order to transact customs business as a broker." — [19 CFR 111.2, Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/19/111.2)
- 19 CFR 111.2(a)(2) exceptions. These are paraphrased by the fetch tool, which would not return the full text verbatim:
  - (i) importers or exporters acting solely on their own account, plus their authorized regular employees or officers acting only for them;
  - (ii) an employee acting solely for a broker, under the broker's POA or named in a statement filed with the processing center, with the broker strictly responsible;
  - (iii) marine transactions (vessel entry or clearance);
  - (iv) transportation in bond by carriers;
  - (v) noncommercial shipments for another party under 19 CFR 141.33;
  - (vi) foreign-trade-zone activity not involving transfer into US customs territory.

  [19 CFR 111.2, Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/19/111.2)
- 19 CFR 111.36(b): a broker generally may not enter into an agreement with an unlicensed person to transact customs business for others "in such manner that the fees or other benefits resulting from the services rendered for others inure to the benefit of the unlicensed person." The only exception, 111.36(c), lets a broker pay a freight forwarder for referrals under conditions. — [19 CFR 111.36, Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/19/111.36)
- The 2022 modernization rule requires a broker to execute its power of attorney directly with the importer of record or drawback claimant, not through a freight forwarder or other third party. CBP gave brokers until 2/17/2023 to fix older POAs. — [CSMS # 54194146 via GovDelivery](https://content.govdelivery.com/accounts/USDHSCBP/bulletins/33aefe2); [CBP Broker Modernization page](https://www.cbp.gov/trade/programs-administration/customs-brokers/modernization)

### Inferences
- **(a) General advice.** No regulation text exempts advice. The definition reaches "activities involving transactions with CBP concerning" classification, valuation, refunds and drawback. CBP's own rulings separate *general* education, which is allowed, from advice on *particular* entries or claims, which is not allowed (see section 6). "Analysis" tied to a specific importer's specific entries for a filing is therefore risky.
- **(b) Preparing for the importer to file.** This is expressly customs business: "preparation... of documents in any format... intended to be filed with CBP... whether or not signed or filed by the preparer." The importer's own-account exception (111.2(a)(2)(i)) covers only the importer and its own regular employees or officers. It does not cover an outside contractor.
- **(c) Preparing for a licensed broker to review and file.** Also customs business, because the preparer still prepares documents intended for filing. The broker-employee exception (111.2(a)(2)(ii)) requires a common-law *employee* of the broker under its supervision, and an independent contractor or separate company does not qualify. If the client pays the unlicensed preparer, 111.36(b) bars the broker from the arrangement (see HQ 113715).
- **(d) Filing on the importer's behalf.** This is customs business, and no exception applies. The "mere electronic transmission" carve-out covers only a pure conduit that transmits data it *received* for transmission. It does not cover someone who selected, built or prepared the data.
- There is no exception for drawback agents or specialists, consultants, auditors or refund firms anywhere in 1641 or 111.2(a)(2).

### Gaps
- The full verbatim text of 111.2(a)(2) could not be retrieved; the fetch tool paraphrased it. Verify the exact wording on eCFR.
- The 2022 final rule's preamble (87 FR 63267) could not be fetched because federalregister.gov blocked it. CBP's comment responses on "corporate compliance activity," consultants and software were therefore not quoted.

## 2. Drawback (19 CFR part 190): who is the claimant, who may file, and is it customs business?

### Takeaway
Drawback is named in the statutory and regulatory definition of customs business, so preparing or filing drawback claims for someone else for pay requires a broker license. Part 190 provides only two filing paths: the claimant itself (its officers, partners, owner, or employees with a POA) or a licensed customs broker with a POA. There is no "agent" or "drawback specialist" category. The researcher found no regulatory basis that makes unlicensed drawback specialists legal when they prepare claims. A long-standing CBP ruling (HQ 114199) says unlicensed consultants "cannot prepare, or assist a client in preparing" documents to be filed.

### Cited Findings
- 19 CFR 190.1: Commissioner of CBP prescribes drawback rules; Treasury "has the sole authority to approve, rules and regulations regarding drawback." — [19 CFR 190.1, Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/19/190.1)
- 19 CFR 190.2 "Drawback claim": "the drawback entry and related documents required by regulation which together constitute the request for drawback payment." It adds: "All drawback claims must be filed electronically through a CBP-authorized Electronic Data Interchange system." "Filing" means "the electronic delivery to CBP of any document or documentation." Part 190.2 does not define "claimant," "agent" or "filer." — [19 CFR 190.2, Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/19/190.2)
- 19 CFR 190.51(a)(2): the drawback entry must be "filed through a CBP-authorized electronic system" and include "Claimant identification number;" and "Broker identification number (if applicable);". 190.51(d): "All drawback claims must be submitted through a CBP-authorized system." — [19 CFR 190.51, Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/19/190.51)
- 19 CFR 190.6(a), who may sign or electronically certify drawback documents (quoted list):
  - "The president, a vice president, secretary, treasurer, or any other employee legally authorized to bind the corporation;"
  - "A full partner of a partnership;"
  - "The owner of a sole proprietorship;"
  - "Any employee of the business entity with a power of attorney;"
  - "An individual acting on his or her own behalf; or"
  - a licensed customs broker with "a power of attorney to sign the applicable drawback document."

  [19 CFR 190.6, Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/19/190.6)
- 19 CFR 190.82: by default "the exporter (or destroyer) will be entitled to claim drawback." The exporter can waive and assign the right "to the manufacturer, producer, importer, or intermediate party." The section does not mention agents or brokers. — [19 CFR 190.82, Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/19/190.82)
- CBP's 2022 broker rulemaking discussion says "Drawback claimants are included in the phrase 'or other party in interest'" and that a broker "must execute and obtain a power of attorney directly from either the importer of record or drawback claimant." The quotes come via search snippet of CBP and Federal Register material; the researcher did not fetch the full document. — [Federal Register 2022-22445 (search snippet)](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2022/10/18/2022-22445/modernization-of-the-customs-broker-regulations); [CBP Broker Modernization page](https://www.cbp.gov/trade/programs-administration/customs-brokers/modernization)
- HQ 114199 (Feb. 26, 1998), addressed to a former Customs employee and unlicensed consultant. Per the ruling text on a mirror site:
  - The consultant "may give instruction or advice to clients on general issues," including "teaching brokers, importers and exporters how to establish a drawback program."
  - The consultant "may not advise brokers or other clients on actual drawback contracts or claims."
  - The consultant "may not offer advice, or participate in discussions with Customs, on issues relating to a particular transaction."
  - The consultant "cannot prepare, or assist a client in preparing, documents and forms intended to be filed with the Customs Service." The one exception is a protest, if "the consultant has been issued a power of attorney to file the protest."

  [HQ 114199 mirror (open-gov.usebase.io)](https://open-gov.usebase.io/rulings/114199)
- HQ 113715 (Jan. 9, 1997). An unlicensed company whose service "results in the discovery of a drawback opportunity" engaged a licensed broker "to prepare and present the drawback claims," paid by the company from client fees. CBP held that this arrangement violates the broker's duty not to deal with unlicensed persons (then 111.36(a), now 111.36(b)), because the unlicensed company "would receive a monetary benefit from the performance of Customs business by the broker for a third party." By contrast, owning shares in a licensed brokerage and receiving dividends was not a violation. — [HQ 113715 mirror (open-gov.usebase.io)](https://open-gov.usebase.io/rulings/113715)
- HQ 114045 reportedly kept that position even with a US attorney between the consultant and the broker. This is from a search-result summary only; the researcher did not fetch the ruling. — [HQ 114045 mirror](https://open-gov.usebase.io/rulings/114045)

### Inferences
- **(a) General advice.** General drawback education, such as how drawback works or how to set up a program, is allowed per HQ 114199. Advice on a specific claimant's actual claims is not.
- **(b) Preparing a claim the claimant files.** This is customs business. HQ 114199 says unlicensed persons cannot "prepare, or assist a client in preparing" filed documents. The claimant can file its own claim: 1641(b)(1) excludes acting "solely on behalf of that person," and 190.6 lets the claimant's officers and employees sign.
- **(c) Preparing a claim a broker reviews and files.** HQ 113715 shows CBP treats the unlicensed party's paid role in a broker-filed drawback claim as violating 111.36. The preparer is not the broker's common-law employee, so 111.2(a)(2)(ii) does not apply. A lawful version is a broker that *employs* the person, or a broker that contracts directly with the claimant and does the preparation itself.
- **(d) Filing on the claimant's behalf.** Only a licensed broker with a POA appears in 190.6. There is no "agent" path for non-brokers.
- **Drawback specialists.** Their legality rests on being licensed brokers or employed by one, on being in-house claimant employees, or on limiting themselves to general education or "corporate compliance activity" for related entities. The researcher found no regulation that creates a separate drawback-specialist status. The 1997 and 1998 rulings predate the 2018 TFTEA modernization and the 2022 Part 111 revision, but the statutory definition they apply is unchanged, and the 2022 rule kept "preparation" in the definition.

### Gaps
- The researcher did not read the 2018 TFTEA drawback final rule (83 FR 64942) preamble for comments about drawback specialists or consultants.
- No 2018-or-later CBP ruling specifically on unlicensed drawback preparers was found. The CROSS search at rulings.cbp.gov was not run directly; the rulings above were read via third-party mirrors.
- The researcher did not verify whether ACE or ABI drawback filing needs a filer code that only brokers or self-filers can obtain.

## 3. Post-summary corrections (PSCs)

### Takeaway
A PSC is a correction to an entry summary covering classification, value or duties, so it falls squarely within "customs business." CBP's page says "The trade is responsible for submitting a PSC" through ACE. An "authorized ACE entry summary filer" may correct an entry another filer submitted if the same importer of record authorizes it. In practice that means the importer, if it self-files, or a licensed broker with a POA. Preparing a PSC for others for pay is customs business under the plain text, for activities (b), (c) and (d).

### Cited Findings
- CBP: "The trade is responsible for submitting a PSC." An "authorized ACE entry summary filer may submit a PSC" for an entry another filer submitted "if authorized by the same importer of record." PSCs may be filed "within 300 days from the date of entry," or up to 15 days before scheduled liquidation, whichever is earlier, and ACE automatically rejects late PSCs. Changing the importer of record requires "The importer or broker must submit a CBP Form 3347." — [CBP Post Summary Correction page](https://cbp.gov/trade/programs-administration/entry-summary/post-summary-correction)
- Older secondary guidance gives eligibility limits: type 01 and 03 entries only, not type 11 informal entries; the entry must be unliquidated, paid, and not under CBP review; and it states a 270-day limit, which conflicts with CBP's current 300-day figure. — [ICPA ACE PSC guide](https://www.icpainc.org/wp-content/uploads/2014/10/ACE-Post-Summary-Correction.pdf); conflicts with [CBP PSC page](https://cbp.gov/trade/programs-administration/entry-summary/post-summary-correction). Prefer CBP's 300 days.
- An "entry summary filer" for others must be a licensed broker under 1641(b)(1); see section 1.

### Inferences
- **(a) General advice.** General guidance on PSCs is low risk. Identifying that a specific entry has a wrong HTS code and telling the importer how to correct it is advice on "a particular transaction" (HQ 114199 framing) and on classification (HQ H350722 framing). That is high risk.
- **(b), (c), (d).** All are customs business for an unlicensed outsider. The importer may self-file a PSC only if it has its own ABI entry-summary filer status; the researcher did not verify the self-filer mechanics. Otherwise its broker files.

### Gaps
- No CBP ruling found that specifically addresses an unlicensed party preparing PSCs.
- The ABI self-filer requirements for PSCs were not verified.

## 4. CAPE IEEPA refund declarations (2026)

### Takeaway
CBP's IEEPA refund page states: "Only the IOR or the licensed customs broker who filed the entries can file a CAPE Declaration." CBP expressly answers "No" to whether an attorney can file for the importer. Declarations go through the ACE Portal, not ABI. Refunds go only to the IOR or the Form 4811 notify party. No CBP text authorizes third-party refund firms to file. Such firms can be paid only through private arrangements with the IOR, and preparing the declaration for the IOR is itself customs business under 1641(a)(2) ("refund... thereof"; "preparation of documents... whether or not signed or filed by the preparer").

### Cited Findings
- CBP IEEPA Duty Refunds page (last modified Sept. 30, 2026):
  - "Only the IOR or the licensed customs broker who filed the entries can file a CAPE Declaration."
  - An attorney cannot file on an importer's behalf; CBP's answer is "No."
  - Brokers may combine entries they filed for multiple IORs on one declaration, up to 9,999 entries.
  - CBP "is only able to refund IEEPA duties to the Importer of Record or the Notify Party."
  - Refund banking requires the "Importer" sub-account in the ACE Portal.
  - CAPE deployed April 20, 2026 (CSMS # 68340863).
  - Phase 1 covered certain unliquidated entries and certain entries within 80 days of liquidation. Phase 2 covers reconciliation-flagged entries (June 29, 2026; CSMS # 69035485), plus warehouse entries (CSMS # 69127837).
  - Other referenced CSMS messages: # 68510928 (account reactivation) and # 68569567 (protecting refund information).
  - The page does not mention consultants, refund firms or Section 122.

  [CBP IEEPA Duty Refunds](https://www.cbp.gov/trade/programs-administration/trade-remedies/ieepa-duty-refunds)
- "Filers do not use the Automated Broker Interface (ABI) to file a CAPE Declaration." Declarations are capped at 9,999 entries each, and multiple declarations are allowed. — [CBP IEEPA Duty Refunds (search snippet)](https://www.cbp.gov/trade/programs-administration/trade-remedies/ieepa-duty-refunds)
- Secondary: ACE validates that the submitter is the IOR or the broker that filed the entry summaries, and rejects the whole declaration if not. — [White and Williams](https://www.whiteandwilliams.com/resources-alerts-IEEPA-Tariff-Refunds-CBP-Launches-CAPE-Process); [GGG LLP](https://www.gggllp.com/how-to-file-for-your-ieepa-tariff-refund-through-the-cape-system/)
- Secondary: Form 4811 directs where a refund is sent but does not transfer ownership of the refund. — [search summary citing Shapiro / Holland & Knight](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/04/cape-has-arrived-navigating-the-next-phase-of-ieepa-duty-refunds)
- Secondary: Phase 3 opens Oct. 6, 2026 for plaintiffs who gave CBP a valid IOR number by July 30, 2026. — [GHY](https://www.ghy.com/trade-compliance/cbp-cape-ieepa-refund-progress/) (not verified against a CSMS message)
- Secondary: on Oct. 8, 2026, the CIT certified a class of importers that paid IEEPA duties. — [Troutman Pepper Locke](https://www.troutman.com/insights/getting-duties-back-where-recovery-stands-under-ieepa-section-122-and-section-301-forced-labor-tariffs/)

### Inferences
- **(a) General advice.** Explaining CAPE, eligibility and phases is general education. Estimating a specific importer's potential refund from its own ACE data is a gray zone. Under HQ 114199 and H350722 reasoning, analysis of particular entries that feeds a filing is risky. Analysis the importer uses only for its own decisions, with no document produced for filing, is the most defensible.
- **(b) Building the CAPE CSV for the IOR to upload.** The CSV is a document "intended to be filed with CBP" concerning a "refund," so preparing it is customs business "whether or not signed or filed by the preparer." Software that lets the importer build its own file from its own data is closer to a tool than to a service. H350722 nonetheless treated OCR extraction of entry data as customs business, so a tool that *selects and prepares* the data for submission carries risk. This is an inference; no ruling on CAPE tools was found.
- **(c) Preparing for the filing broker to submit.** Only the broker "who filed the entries" may file, and the unlicensed preparer has no exception. 111.36(b) applies if the preparer is paid from the refund.
- **(d) Filing for the IOR.** CBP's page says this is not allowed for anyone except the IOR or the filing broker, not even attorneys. Logging into the IOR's ACE Portal as a non-employee user to file would put the filer outside the own-account exception (111.2(a)(2)(i) covers the importer's "authorized regular employees or officers").

### Gaps
- No Federal Register notice on CAPE filer eligibility was reached. One secondary site mentions a July 2026 FR notice, but it was unverified.
- No CBP statement found that addresses whether an IOR's TAO may give an ACE Portal user account to a non-employee consultant.
- The Phase 3 CSMS message was not reviewed directly.

## 5. Section 122 refund claims

### Takeaway
In May 2026 the CIT held, 2-1, that the 10% Section 122 global tariff (Proclamation 11012) exceeded the president's authority. Relief was limited to the plaintiffs and the State of Washington. The Federal Circuit issued an administrative stay on May 12, 2026, and the appeal is pending. CAPE covers IEEPA duties only, and the researcher found no CBP mechanism for Section 122 refunds. Filing mechanics would currently run through litigation, or protests or PSCs by the IOR or its broker, which raises the same licensing analysis.

### Cited Findings
- The CIT held 2-1 that the Section 122 tariff exceeded presidential authority and granted summary judgment to the plaintiffs. — [Gibson Dunn](https://www.gibsondunn.com/section-122-global-tariffs-invalidated-by-the-court-of-international-trade-ruling-and-next-steps/)
- Relief is limited to the plaintiffs and Washington; there is no nationwide injunction. — [Snell & Wilmer](https://www.swlaw.com/publication/more-tariff-refunds-not-so-fast-court-of-international-trade-strikes-down-section-122-tariffs-but-relief-is-limited-to-plaintiffs/)
- The Federal Circuit issued an administrative stay May 12, and the stay motion was pending. — [PwC](https://www.pwc.com/ca/en/services/tax/publications/tax-insights/us-court-strike-down-section-122-tariffs-2026.html); [Skadden](https://www.skadden.com/insights/publications/2026/05/us-trade-court-strikes-down-section-122-tariffs)
- The CBP IEEPA refund page does not mention Section 122. — [CBP IEEPA Duty Refunds](https://www.cbp.gov/trade/programs-administration/trade-remedies/ieepa-duty-refunds)

### Inferences
- Any Section 122 refund document filed with CBP, such as a protest or PSC, is "refund" customs business under 1641(a)(2). The own-account and broker paths apply as above. Per HQ 114199, protests are a special case: the ruling notes a consultant may file a protest with a POA. Verify this against 19 CFR 174.3, which the researcher did not check.

### Gaps
- Status after July 2026 is unverified: the appeal outcome, whether the stay was granted, and the expiry and refund handling. No October 2026 source was found.
- The protest-filing rules for non-brokers under 19 CFR 174.3 were not checked.

## 6. CBP rulings on "customs business" for consultants, software providers and refund or audit firms

### Takeaway
CBP's rulings consistently allow general education and pure referrals. They do not allow advice on particular transactions, preparing or extracting data for filing, or giving 8- or 10-digit HTS codes for goods to be imported. They also bar fee arrangements where an unlicensed party profits from a broker's work. HQ H350722 (Jan. 16, 2026) applies this framework to a digital platform, including AI classification, OCR data extraction and Form 5106 filing.

### Cited Findings
- HQ H350722 (Jan. 16, 2026), per law-firm summaries (secondary; the ruling PDF could not be text-extracted):
  - Submitting CBP Form 5106 for importers is customs business because it "directly supports the entry process."
  - OCR extraction of entry data "inherently constitutes customs business."
  - Generating "eight- or ten-digit tariff subheadings" for goods intended for import "effectively performs a licensed function without authorization." There is limited flexibility for general guidance that is independent of broker services and carries a meaningful disclaimer.
  - CBP said "importers and brokers must handle POA execution directly, without intermediaries."
  - Referral-type broker connections are allowed.

  [Barnes Richardson](https://www.barnesrichardson.com/cbp-ruling-clarifies-customs-business-addresses-ai); [Diaz Trade Law](https://diaztradelaw.com/even-ai-needs-a-license-know-when-automation-unlawfully-crosses-into-customs-business/); [Green Worldwide](https://www.greenworldwide.com/cbp-defines-limits-of-unlicensed-digital-platforms-in-ruling-hq-h350722/); ruling PDF: [rulings.cbp.gov H350722](https://rulings.cbp.gov/api/getdoc/hq/2026/H350722.pdf)
- Diaz Trade Law quotes the ruling: "the actual decision regarding the classification of imported merchandise must be made by a duly licensed customs broker." H350722 relies on HQ H290535 (2022), where a disclaimer did not save a company that gave specific subheadings for goods being imported. — [Diaz Trade Law, via search summary](https://diaztradelaw.com/even-ai-needs-a-license-know-when-automation-unlawfully-crosses-into-customs-business/)
- HQ 114199 (1998) draws the general-versus-particular line for drawback consultants (see section 2). — [HQ 114199 mirror](https://open-gov.usebase.io/rulings/114199)
- HQ 113715 (1997) holds that a paid unlicensed drawback-discovery consultant arranging broker-filed claims causes a 111.36 violation. — [HQ 113715 mirror](https://open-gov.usebase.io/rulings/113715)
- HQ H097816, per search summary, found a data-referral arrangement acceptable because the unlicensed party was not using the data to prepare customs documents. This was not fetched. — [tarifflens mirror (403 on fetch)](https://www.tarifflens.ai/rulings/H097816)

### Inferences
- A contingency-fee refund or audit firm that finds refund opportunities in an importer's entries and then has a broker file is structurally the HQ 113715 scenario. CBP held that impermissible for the broker.
- A software product that shows an importer its own data and potential refund eligibility looks safest when it does not generate the filing document, does not give entry-specific classification determinations, and carries a disclaimer. Under H350722, extracting or preparing filing data and giving entry-specific 8- or 10-digit codes cross the line.

### Gaps
- The full text of H350722 and H290535 was not read directly.
- No ruling specific to CAPE, PSC preparation or refund-audit firms after 2022 was found.
- CROSS was not searched directly.

## 7. Penalties for unlicensed customs business

### Takeaway
The statutory penalty is up to $10,000 per transaction for "intentionally" transacting customs business for others without a license. The regulation adds an aggregate cap of $30,000.

### Cited Findings
- 19 U.S.C. 1641(b)(6): "Any person who intentionally transacts customs business, other than solely on the behalf of that person, without holding a valid customs broker's license granted to that person under this subsection shall be liable to the United States for a monetary penalty not to exceed $10,000 for each such transaction as well as for each violation of any other provision of this section." — [19 U.S.C. 1641, Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/19/1641)
- 19 CFR 111.4: "Any person who intentionally transacts customs business," other than as provided in 111.2(a)(2), without "a valid broker's license" "will be liable for a monetary penalty for each such transaction" and for "each violation of any other provision of 19 U.S.C. 1641." "The penalty will be assessed in accordance with subpart E of this part." — [19 CFR 111.4, Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/19/111.4)
- 19 CFR 111.91(b): for a person who is not a broker, the penalty is up to "$10,000 for each transaction or violation referred to in § 111.4," with an aggregate of "$30,000 for all those transactions or violations." — [19 CFR 111.91, Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/19/111.91)
- The licensing rules also reach the broker. Brokers face their own penalty, up to an aggregate of $30,000 under 111.91(a), and possible suspension or revocation for violating 111.36. — [19 CFR 111.91, Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/19/111.91); [19 CFR 111.36, Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/19/111.36)

### Inferences
- The "intentionally" scienter requirement limits penalty exposure. Still, a business built around a contested activity cannot easily claim the conduct was unintentional after CBP has published rulings on it.

### Gaps
- The researcher did not verify whether the $10,000 and $30,000 figures are inflation-adjusted under 19 CFR 171 or the Federal Civil Penalties Inflation Adjustment Act.
- The researcher did not check whether the $30,000 aggregate cap in 111.91(b) is applied per proceeding or overall.
- No enforcement data on penalties actually assessed against unlicensed persons was found.
