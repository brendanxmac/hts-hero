# Broker referral fees, fee sharing and platform arrangements with an unlicensed tariff software company (19 CFR Part 111)

Scope: US law as of 2026-10-16. Regulation text below was pulled verbatim from the eCFR versioner API for the 2026-10-01 point-in-time version of 19 CFR (Part 111's latest amendment date in eCFR is 2023-07-24, so the post-2022 text is current). CBP rulings were pulled in full text from the CROSS API (rulings.cbp.gov). The 2022 final rule preamble was pulled from the GPO text of 87 FR 63267 (FR Doc 2022-22445).

Source URLs used throughout:
- eCFR 19 CFR Part 111: https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-111
- 87 FR 63267 (Oct. 18, 2022), CBP Dec. 22-21: https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2022/10/18/2022-22445/modernization-of-the-customs-broker-regulations

## 1. Exact text of 19 CFR 111.36 and whether a referral fee to an unlicensed lead source violates it

### Takeaway
A tariff software company is not a "freight forwarder," so the only carve-out (111.36(c)) does not apply. Under 111.36(b), CBP consistently says a broker may not pay an unlicensed non-employee a commission, a percentage, or a per-importer or per-shipment referral or finder's fee tied to the broker's customs business. CBP has said only that a flat fee "not tied to any particular transaction" would be permissible. In its 2022 preamble CBP rejected a carve-out for unlicensed sales representatives, and in January 2026 it applied the same rule to an online platform that connects importers to brokers (H350722).

### Cited Findings
**Current verbatim text of § 111.36 (as amended by CBP Dec. 22-21, 87 FR 63318):** [eCFR 111.36](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-111/subpart-C/section-111.36)
> (a) Employment by unlicensed person other than importer. When a broker is employed for the transaction of customs business by an unlicensed person who is not the actual importer, the broker must transmit to the actual importer either a copy of his bill for services rendered or a copy of the entry, unless the merchandise was purchased on a delivered duty-paid basis or unless the importer has in writing waived transmittal of the copy of the entry or bill for services rendered.
>
> (b) Service to others not to benefit unlicensed person. Except as otherwise provided in paragraph (c) of this section, a broker must not enter into any agreement with an unlicensed person to transact customs business for others in such manner that the fees or other benefits resulting from the services rendered for others inure to the benefit of the unlicensed person.
>
> (c) Relations with a freight forwarder. A broker may compensate a freight forwarder for referring brokerage business, subject to the following conditions:
> (1) The importer or other party in interest is notified in advance by the forwarder or broker of the name of the broker selected by the forwarder for the handling of his Customs transactions;
> (2) The broker transmits directly to the importer or other party in interest: (i) A true copy of his brokerage charges if the fees and charges are to be collected by or through the forwarder, unless this requirement is waived in writing by the importer or other party in interest; or (ii) A statement of his brokerage charges and an itemized list of any charges to be collected for the account of the freight forwarder if the fees and charges are to be collected by or through the broker;
> (3) The broker must execute a customs power of attorney directly with the importer of record or drawback claimant, and not via a freight forwarder or other third party, to transact customs business for that importer of record or drawback claimant. No part of the agreement of compensation between the broker and the forwarder, nor any action taken pursuant to the agreement, can forbid or prevent direct communication between the importer of record, drawback claimant, or other party in interest and the broker; and
> (4) In making the agreement and in all actions taken pursuant to the agreement, the broker remains subject to all other provisions of this part.
> [T.D. 00-17, 65 FR 13891, Mar. 15, 2000, as amended by CBP Dec. 22-21, 87 FR 63318, Oct. 18, 2022]

- The 2022 rule changed only paragraph (c)(3) of § 111.36 ("In § 111.36, revise paragraph (c)(3) to read as follows"). Paragraphs (a), (b), (c)(1), (c)(2) and (c)(4) were left as they were — [87 FR 63267, amendatory instruction 23](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2022/10/18/2022-22445/modernization-of-the-customs-broker-regulations)
- Definition, § 111.1: "Freight forwarder" means "a person engaged in the business of dispatching shipments in foreign commerce between the United States, its territories or possessions, and foreign countries, and handling the formalities incident to such shipments, on behalf of other persons." — [eCFR 111.1](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-111/subpart-A/section-111.1)
- Definition, § 111.1: "Customs business" means "those activities involving transactions with CBP concerning the entry and admissibility of merchandise, its classification and valuation, the payment of duties, taxes, or other charges assessed or collected by CBP on merchandise by reason of its importation, and the refund, rebate, or drawback of those duties, taxes, or other charges." It also covers "the preparation, and activities relating to the preparation, of documents in any format and the electronic transmission of documents and parts of documents intended to be filed with CBP in furtherance of any other customs business activity, whether or not signed or filed by the preparer." It excludes "the mere electronic transmission of data received for transmission to CBP" and "a corporate compliance activity." Refund and drawback work therefore falls inside "customs business." — [eCFR 111.1](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-111/subpart-A/section-111.1)
- **2022 preamble, CBP rejecting a sales-rep carve-out (87 FR 63289):** A commenter asked CBP to add to (b) that brokers may compensate sales representatives "in a manner that is agreeable to both." CBP refused: "Such an arrangement would prevent transparency of the billing of services rendered and goes against the overarching principle that brokers must not share fees generated from customs business with unlicensed parties." — [87 FR 63289](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2022/10/18/2022-22445/modernization-of-the-customs-broker-regulations)
- **2022 preamble, CBP rejecting repeal of fee-splitting (87 FR 63289–90):** "Brokers are prohibited from creating fee arrangements whereby the fees or other benefits resulting from the customs business services rendered by a broker will directly benefit an unlicensed person or entity. Thus, agreements wherein unlicensed persons acting as independent agents receive a commission for marketing or selling customs services on behalf of a brokerage company are generally prohibited." Also: "Commission payments to an employee are permitted, but not to independent agents ... Instead, a flat fee, not tied to a particular transaction, would be permissible to compensate third-party agents for selling customs services." — [87 FR 63290](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2022/10/18/2022-22445/modernization-of-the-customs-broker-regulations)
- **2022 preamble (87 FR 63288):** CBP "does strictly ensure that persons not actually employed or supervised by a broker do not get paid a portion of the fee derived from customs business services; such persons may instead be paid by a flat fee." — [87 FR 63288](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2022/10/18/2022-22445/modernization-of-the-customs-broker-regulations)
- **HQ 113965 (June 6, 1997):** "Unlicensed independent agents ... may not accept commissions from CTS for promoting CTS's brokerage services." Reasoning: "as a general rule, commissions are a percentage of the fee paid for [customs] services rendered." On the alternative: "It is possible that a different payment method, such as a flat amount paid by CTS to its agents that is not tied to any particular transaction, would not trigger the regulation." Licensed independent agents may take commissions if their activities are not customs business. — [HQ 113965](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/113965)
- **HQ H276784 (Dec. 29, 2016), Welke:** The broker proposed paying two non-profits (World Trade Center Buffalo Niagara and COSE) a referral or "finder's fee," described as "a commission plan based fee that is calculated as a percentage of the entry fee," paid "per importer or per shipment" for up to five years. It was proposed both with and without a formal agreement. Held: "Unlicensed independent agents, may not accept commissions from Welke for promoting its brokerage services per 19 C.F.R. § 111.36." The importer was billed directly and had direct communication with the broker, and that did not cure the problem. — [HQ H276784](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/H276784)
- **HQ H290002 (Nov. 30, 2018), modified by HQ H302355 (Jan. 29, 2019):** A broker may pay its own unlicensed employees commissions. It may not pay commissions to a sister company's employees, because they are "unlicensed independent agents who may not receive a benefit derived from fees earned by WUSA's transaction of customs business." On solicitation: unlicensed non-employees may solicit for a broker "subject to adequate supervision," and the broker should make sure they are "directly warned not to perform any task on its behalf that may be considered customs business." Violations "may result in the imposition of penalties or other sanctions pursuant to 19 U.S.C. § 1641(b)(6) and/or 19 U.S.C. § 1641(d)(1)(C)." — [HQ H302355](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/H302355); [HQ H290002](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/H290002)
- **HQ H350722 (Jan. 16, 2026), unlicensed online platform:** The platform connects importers to third-party brokers, has an OCR tool, has an AI classification tool, and filed CBP Form 5106 for clients. CBP said: "CBP has repeatedly held that unlicensed entities other than a freight forwarder may not receive fees or other compensation such as a commission from a broker if such benefits are tied to particular customs business transactions performed by a broker." On the platform's own fee: "We caution that such an arrangement may violate 19 C.F.R. § 111.36(b) if the fee paid to the Unlicensed Company by an importer seeking to engage a third-party broker is tied to the entry filed by the broker." — [HQ H350722](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/H350722)
- **HQ 113715 (Jan. 9, 1997):** "gratuitous, informal business referrals to the brokerage firm do not constitute the 'agreement' contemplated by § 111.36(a)." In Scenario 2, the unlicensed referrer owned shares in a broker and received ordinary dividends with no fees or commissions on referred business, and CBP found no violation. — [HQ 113715](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/113715)
- **HQ 114404 (Mar. 16, 1999), DuPont:** "Although DuPont may refer importers to DIO [a licensed broker], it may not benefit from the services rendered as a result of the referral." — [HQ 114404](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/114404)
- **Forwarders only, HQ H135395 (Mar. 23, 2011):** For a qualifying freight forwarder, "The regulations are silent as to whether there are restrictions on the type of compensation agreement ... you are permitted to negotiate a compensation agreement based on a flat rate or a percentage." Per-referral payments ($10 per referral in HQ 115080, 2000) are allowed for forwarders when the (c) conditions are met. — [HQ H135395](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/H135395); [HQ 115080](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/115080)
- If a forwarder refers without being paid, 111.36(c) does not apply: "the regulation is expressly limited in scope to the conditions of a freight forwarder's compensation by a broker." — [HQ H318461 (Aug. 25, 2022)](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/H318461)
- **Related rule, § 111.37 Misuse of license or permit:** "A broker must not allow his license, permit or name to be used by or for any unlicensed person (including a broker whose license or permit is under suspension), other than his own employees authorized to act for him, in the solicitation, promotion or performance of any customs business or transaction." — [eCFR 111.37](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-111/subpart-C/section-111.37)
- **Related rule, § 111.42(a)(4):** A broker must not "Share fees with" any notoriously disreputable person or person whose license was revoked, is suspended, or was cancelled with prejudice. This is the only Part 111 rule that uses the phrase "share fees." The general bar is in 111.36(b). — [eCFR 111.42](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-111/subpart-C/section-111.42)

### Inferences
- A percentage-of-fee revenue share, a per-shipment fee, or a per-entry fee paid by a broker to the software company is prohibited under 111.36(b) on CBP's consistent reading (H276784 and H350722 are directly on point). It does not matter whether the broker bills the importer directly or whether the importer talks to the broker directly. In H276784 both were true and the arrangement was still disallowed.
- A one-time "bounty" per referred importer that signs up is very likely treated the same way. H276784 rejected a fee "per importer." The forwarder-only per-referral fee in HQ 115080 is permitted because the (c) exception applies. No ruling was found approving a per-client bounty to a non-forwarder.
- The arrangement CBP has signalled as acceptable is a flat fee "not tied to any particular transaction," such as a fixed monthly marketing, sponsorship, advertising or listing fee that does not vary with referrals, entries or refunds. No ruling squarely approves a specific flat-fee platform deal. The language is dicta ("possible," "would be permissible"), so a binding ruling under 19 CFR Part 177 is the way to get certainty.
- Unpaid referrals (a "find a broker" link with no money flowing back) are not an "agreement" under 111.36(b) (HQ 113715, HQ H318461).
- Holding equity in a broker and receiving ordinary dividends, with no per-referral payment, was approved in HQ 113715 Scenario 2. Any outside "principal" with a 5% or greater interest triggers § 111.28(e) notice and possible background checks.
- Refund and drawback work is "customs business" by definition, so revenue sharing on broker-filed refund claims, post-summary corrections or drawback is covered by 111.36(b) just like entries.
- Protests are a possible nuance. CBP held that an unlicensed consultant preparing and filing protests under a 19 CFR 174.3 power of attorney is not conducting "customs business" (HQ H272798). It is therefore arguable whether a broker's protest-only fee is "customs business" whose fees cannot inure to an unlicensed person. No ruling addresses fee sharing on protests specifically. Treat it as unresolved and conservatively assume 111.36(b) applies.

### Gaps
- No CBP ruling found approving a flat-fee or subscription marketing arrangement between a broker and a software or lead-generation platform on specific facts. Only dicta.
- Published penalty cases or broker suspensions based specifically on 111.36(b) were not found in this pass.
- How Flexport, Zonos, Customs City and similar platforms actually structure broker pay was not researched (no primary sources reached). Flexport holds its own broker license, so its arrangements may involve no unlicensed party; this is unverified here.
- The Broker Management Handbook (referenced in the 2022 preamble re "Broker A–Broker B") was not retrieved.

## 2. Other broker duty rules that touch referral, marketing and platform arrangements

### Takeaway
Beyond 111.36, the rules that matter are:
- the direct POA requirement in 111.36(c)(3), which CBP applies to any third party, including platforms
- client-record confidentiality (111.24)
- no use of the broker's name or license by unlicensed persons (111.37)
- responsible supervision and control (111.28)
- customs business only within US customs territory (111.3(a))
- honest and diligent client advice (111.39, 111.32, 111.38)
- the 111.29 payment notices

A statutory know-your-client mandate exists in 19 U.S.C. 1641(i), but the implementing rule is still only proposed.

### Cited Findings
- **§ 111.28(a):** Every licensed broker or officer "must exercise responsible supervision and control (see § 111.1) over the transaction of the customs business." Factors include "(9) The extent to which the individually licensed broker who qualifies the permit is involved in the operation of the brokerage and communications between CBP and the brokerage" and "(10) Any circumstances which indicate that an individually licensed broker has a real interest in the operations of a brokerage." — [eCFR 111.28](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-111/subpart-C/section-111.28)
- **§ 111.28(e):** A "principal" is "any person having at least a five (5) percent capital, beneficiary or other direct or indirect interest in the business of a broker." Changes in ownership require immediate written notice, and CBP may background-check the new principal. — [eCFR 111.28](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-111/subpart-C/section-111.28)
- **§ 111.1 definition:** "Responsible supervision and control" means "that degree of supervision and control necessary to ensure the proper transaction of the customs business of a broker." — [eCFR 111.1](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-111/subpart-A/section-111.1)
- **§ 111.29(a):** "Each broker must exercise due diligence in making financial settlements, in answering correspondence, and in preparing or assisting in the preparation and filing of records relating to any customs business matter handled by him as a broker." Within 60 days the broker must give a written statement accounting for funds received for the client from the Government, among other funds. — [eCFR 111.29](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-111/subpart-C/section-111.29)
- **§ 111.29(b)(1):** Every broker must give clients this written notice: "If you are the importer of record, payment to the broker will not relieve you of liability for customs charges (duties, taxes, or other debts owed CBP) in the event the charges are not paid by the broker. Therefore, if you pay by check, customs charges may be paid with a separate check payable to the 'U.S. Customs and Border Protection' which will be delivered to CBP by the broker." It must appear on or with the POA and be repeated at least every 12 months to active clients. — [eCFR 111.29](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-111/subpart-C/section-111.29)
- **§ 111.32 False information:** "A broker must not file or procure or assist in the filing of any claim, or of any document, affidavit, or other papers, known by such broker to be false." The broker must also report to CBP when it drops a client that is "intentionally attempting to use the broker to defraud the U.S. Government." — [eCFR 111.32](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-111/subpart-C/section-111.32)
- **§ 111.38:** "A broker must not knowingly use false or misleading representations to procure employment in any customs matter." This bears on co-marketing claims about refunds. — [eCFR 111.38](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-111/subpart-C/section-111.38)
- **§ 111.39:**
  - (a) "A broker must not withhold information from a client relative to any customs business it conducts on behalf of a client who is entitled to the information. The broker must not knowingly impart to a client false information relative to any customs business."
  - (b) "A broker must exercise due diligence to ascertain the correctness of any information which the broker imparts to a client, including advice to the client on the proper payment of any duty."
  - (c) The broker must advise the client "promptly" of known noncompliance or errors, advise "on the proper corrective actions required and retain a record of the broker's communication."
  - (d) No suggesting "any illegal plan for evading payment of any duty."
  - Source: [eCFR 111.39](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-111/subpart-C/section-111.39)
- **§ 111.24 Records confidential:** The broker "must not disclose their contents or any information connected with the records to any persons other than those clients, their surety on a particular entry, and representatives of the Department of Homeland Security ... except on subpoena or court order ... or when authorized in writing by the client." — [eCFR 111.24](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-111/subpart-C/section-111.24)
- **§ 111.3(a):** "Customs business must be conducted within the customs territory of the United States as defined in § 101.1 of this chapter." — [eCFR 111.3](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-111/subpart-A/section-111.3)
- **§ 111.40:** "A broker must not act on behalf of any person, or attempt to represent any person, regarding any protest unless he is authorized to do so in accordance with part 174 of this chapter." — [eCFR 111.40](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-111/subpart-C/section-111.40)
- **§ 141.46:** "Before transacting Customs business in the name of his principal, a customhouse broker is required to obtain a valid power of attorney to do so." — [eCFR 141.46](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-141/subpart-C/section-141.46)
- **The POA must be broker–client, direct (87 FR 63289):** "A power of attorney must be executed between the importer of record or drawback claimant and the broker. A power of attorney cannot be executed between the importer of record or drawback claimant and the freight forwarder who in turn assigns the power of attorney to a broker." CBP grounds this in TFTEA § 116 / 19 U.S.C. 1641(i) and "the notion that a broker should know his or her client." — [87 FR 63289](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2022/10/18/2022-22445/modernization-of-the-customs-broker-regulations)
- **Third parties may still communicate (87 FR 63290):** "there is no prohibition on the communication between the broker and third parties that the client has designated, but there is a prohibition on brokers executing a power of attorney with a third party acting as an intermediary instead of directly with the client." — [87 FR 63290](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2022/10/18/2022-22445/modernization-of-the-customs-broker-regulations)
- **Purpose of (c)(3) (87 FR 63288):** "to clarify that a freight forwarder cannot serve as a barrier to communications between the broker and importer or drawback claimant, to address issues of identity theft, supply chain security, fee transparency, and to help ensure that an unlicensed person is not benefitting from the customs business conducted by the broker." A forwarder "may be included as a third party in a power of attorney between the broker and the importer." — [87 FR 63288](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2022/10/18/2022-22445/modernization-of-the-customs-broker-regulations)
- **E-signatures:** Whether an e-signature is valid on a broker POA "is determined by the applicable state's law" (citing HQ H297978, July 16, 2021). — [87 FR 63289](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2022/10/18/2022-22445/modernization-of-the-customs-broker-regulations)
- **Statute:** 19 U.S.C. 1641 requires "A customs broker shall exercise responsible supervision and control over the customs business that it conducts." It also directs that "The Secretary shall prescribe regulations setting forth the minimum standards for customs brokers and importers, including nonresident importers, regarding the identity of the importer." — [19 U.S.C. 1641 (LII)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/19/1641)
- **KYC rule status:** The only Federal Register document found for "Customs Broker Verification of an Importer's Identity" is the proposed rule of Aug. 14, 2019 (FR Doc 2019-17179). No final rule was returned by the FR API search. — [84 FR, 2019-17179](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2019/08/14/2019-17179/customs-broker-verification-of-an-importers-identity)
- **Penalties, § 111.91:**
  - (a) For a broker: "an amount not to exceed an aggregate of $30,000" for § 111.53 grounds.
  - (b) For a non-broker: "not to exceed $10,000 for each transaction or violation referred to in § 111.4 and in an amount not to exceed an aggregate of $30,000."
  - § 111.53(c) makes violating "the rules or regulations issued under any provision of any law enforced by CBP" a ground for suspension or revocation.
  - § 111.53(d) covers a broker who has "knowingly aided or abetted the violations by any other person."
  - Sources: [eCFR 111.91](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-111/subpart-E/section-111.91); [eCFR 111.53](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-111/subpart-D/section-111.53)
- **§ 111.4:** "Any person who intentionally transacts customs business ... without holding a valid broker's license, will be liable for a monetary penalty for each such transaction." — [eCFR 111.4](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-111/subpart-A/section-111.4)

### Inferences
- The POA "directly" language sits textually inside the forwarder paragraph (c)(3). CBP nonetheless reads it as a general rule: the broker cannot obtain a POA "from someone other than an importer," and no "other third party" may act as intermediary. H350722 applies it to an online platform (see Q4). Treat it as applying to any software-company arrangement.
- Rule 111.38 (no misleading representations to procure employment) and 111.39 apply to joint marketing. Refund-savings claims published jointly by a broker and a software company must be accurate, which fits the project's "savings are potential" rule.

### Gaps
- The text of the 2019 KYC proposed rule and its current status (withdrawn, pending, or folded into another rulemaking) was not checked beyond the FR API listing. Its status should be confirmed on the Unified Agenda.

## 3. Can the unlicensed company bill the importer for its own analysis or software while the broker bills separately? What disclosures are needed?

### Takeaway
Yes, if two conditions hold:
- The software company's own service is genuinely not "customs business."
- Its fee is not tied to, and does not come out of, the broker's customs-business fees.

Separate billing does not cure a fee that is effectively a cut of the broker's work. HQ 221330 and HQ 113715 Scenario 1, a drawback consultant arrangement, were found to violate 111.36. The bigger risk is that the "analysis" itself becomes customs business. Examples: 10-digit classifications for goods being imported, pre-filling entry or refund data, or filing CBP Form 5106.

### Cited Findings
- **HQ 113715 Scenario 1, drawback consultant:** An unlicensed consultant's "service results in the discovery of a drawback opportunity. You engage a licensed customs broker to prepare and present the drawback claims ... your client, the U.S. importer, signs the required 'Power of Attorney' ... The licensed broker then works directly with the importer ... Payment for the broker's service comes from you." Held "violative of § 111.36(a)" (now (b)). — [HQ 113715](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/113715)
- **HQ 221330 (May 20, 1991), as described in HQ 113715:** An unlicensed consultant found drawback eligibility and forwarded documents to a broker. "The consultant billed the importer/drawback claimant, and the broker billed the consultant." CBP's view was that this violated the rule because the consultant "would receive a monetary benefit from the performance of Customs business by the broker for a third party." — [HQ 113715](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/113715)
- **HQ H350722 (2026), platform fee:** A platform fee charged to importers "may violate 19 C.F.R. § 111.36(b) if the fee paid to the Unlicensed Company by an importer seeking to engage a third-party broker is tied to the entry filed by the broker." — [HQ H350722](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/H350722)
- **HQ H350722, classification:** If an AI tool derives "subheadings beyond the six-digit level ... for merchandise that has been imported, or is intended to be imported, then customs business is being impermissibly conducted." The ruling relies on HQ H260075 (6 digits for ISF is not customs business) and HQ 114654: unlicensed persons "may not, however, advise a client on how to classify, appraise, or mark merchandise that is going to be the subject of an entry." — [HQ H350722](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/H350722)
- **HQ H350722, general database exception:** A general classification database with a "meaningfully implemented" disclaimer, available "irrespective of whether [a] particular product is ever actually imported," is "narrowly permissible" (citing HQ H272798). The tool must "operate[] separately from the portal connecting importers to brokers." — [HQ H350722](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/H350722)
- **HQ H290535 (Sept. 29, 2022):** Giving customers 8- and 10-digit subheadings for goods they will import, even with an "information only" disclaimer, "violates 19 U.S.C. § 1641(b)(1) by conducting customs business without a license." — [HQ H290535](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/H290535)
- **HQ H350722, OCR and pre-fill:** "whether data is extracted manually or automatically though an OCR tool, CBP has repeatedly held that an unlicensed entity cannot decide what data should appear on an entry" (citing HQ H326926 (2023) and HQ H068278 (2009)). Also: "A tool does not constitute a 'person' ... the actual decision regarding the classification of imported merchandise, or any other information needed to make entry, must be made by a duly licensed customs broker." — [HQ H350722](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/H350722)
- **HQ H350722, Form 5106:** "only a licensed customs broker may complete and submit CBP Form 5106 on behalf of another party." — [HQ H350722](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/H350722)
- **HQ H272798 (Jan. 26, 2017):** An unlicensed consultant is not conducting customs business by "preparing ruling requests and protests, assisting in the preparation of corporate import manuals and desk procedures, conducting post-entry audit reviews (to the extent the reviews will not lead to the preparation of Customs documents or result in activities leading to the filing of such documents), and filing prior disclosures." On protests, under 19 CFR 174.3 "an unlicensed consultant can prepare, sign, and file a protest if a properly executed power of attorney has been obtained." — [HQ H272798](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/H272798)
- **19 CFR 174.3(a):** When a protest is filed by an agent "other than an attorney at law or a customhouse broker," a POA "which either specifically authorizes such agent to make, sign, and file the protest or grants unlimited authority to such agent" must be filed. — [eCFR 174.3](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-174/subpart-B/section-174.3)
- **Intermediary collection of payments is allowed (HQ H318461, citing HQ H258556):** An intermediary may collect funds from the importer and pass them on, but "only a licensed broker or the importer of record may tender the payment to CBP." The intermediary "may not actively participate in the customs decisions, calculation, or remittance of customs payments." — [HQ H318461](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/H318461)
- **Disclosures:**
  - § 111.36(a): when an unlicensed non-importer employs the broker, the broker must send the actual importer a copy of its bill or the entry, unless the goods are DDP or the importer waives in writing.
  - § 111.29(b): the payment-liability notice.
  - § 111.29(a): 60-day accounting for funds.
  - § 111.24: written client consent before the broker shares any client records with the software company. General CBP Form 5291 POA language "is insufficient to authorize sharing" (HQ H318461, citing H258556). Specific POA consent language was accepted in H318461.
  - Sources: [eCFR 111.36](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-111/subpart-C/section-111.36); [eCFR 111.29](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-111/subpart-C/section-111.29); [HQ H318461](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/H318461)
- **Importer self-disclosure is outside 111.24:** "if an importer is disclosing entry data and documentation to the Unlicensed Company by uploading such information to the online platform ... no violation of 19 C.F.R. § 111.24 occurs because the regulation does not constrain an importer's ability to divulge its own records." — [HQ H350722](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/H350722)

### Inferences
- A structure that fits the rulings looks like this:
  - The software company charges the importer a fee for its own non-customs-business product, such as a subscription. The fee is not contingent on, or priced as a share of, any entry, refund, protest or drawback the broker files.
  - The broker separately bills the importer for its customs-business work.
  - No money flows from the broker to the software company, other than possibly a flat fee not tied to transactions.
- Success fees are a specific risk. If the software company charges the importer "X% of the refund recovered" and the refund is actually obtained through a broker's PSC, protest or drawback filing, the platform's fee is plausibly "tied to" the broker's customs business (H350722) and functionally resembles HQ 221330. That is an inference; no ruling squarely addresses a refund-contingent platform fee.
- For an HTS-classification product, any feature that hands an importer or its broker 10-digit HTS numbers for goods being imported, inside the same flow that routes them to a broker, is squarely the risk CBP flagged in H350722 and H290535. Disclaimers alone do not cure it.

### Gaps
- No ruling found on a software company charging importers a contingency fee on refunds that a broker files.
- HQ 221330 and HQ 225023 were not pulled in full. They are described here only via later rulings.

## 4. Can a software company embed or white-label a broker's filing service?

### Takeaway
Embedding a licensed broker's service is permissible if the platform is a "mere transmission" pipe, and H350722 is the closest authority. The broker must:
- execute the POA directly with the importer, with no platform intermediary in the POA
- make every customs decision itself, including classification and data elements
- keep confidentiality, sharing client records with the platform only with written consent
- exercise responsible supervision and control
- conduct the business inside US customs territory

"White-label" in the sense of the platform's brand fronting the customs service raises 111.37 (no use of the broker's name or license by unlicensed persons) and 111.30(c) (trade names need CBP approval). Platform pay must still avoid transaction-linked fees.

### Cited Findings
- **HQ H350722, platform as intermediary:** A platform where importers upload documents, sign a POA, and chat with a broker is "not impermissibly conducting customs business" because it "does not participate in the importer's uploading of documents and data to the platform, or act as intermediary in communication regarding the entry, nor participate in making entry" and "has no role in deciding what is transmitted." — [HQ H350722](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/H350722)
- **HQ H350722, POA:** "The Unlicensed Company may not act as an intermediary between a broker and importer in the formation or execution of a POA ... a third-party broker engaged by the Unlicensed Company may be violating 19 C.F.R. § 111.36(c)(3) if an importer is not directly receiving a POA from the broker, if the broker is not directly receiving the executed POA from the importer, or if the Unlicensed Company is acting as an intermediary in the execution of the POA." — [HQ H350722](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/H350722)
- **HQ H350722, chat confidentiality:** "if a third-party broker discloses confidential client information to the Unlicensed Company by communicating with importers through the chat function embedded into the online platform, this would violate 19 C.F.R. § 111.24 unless the importer provided written authorization." — [HQ H350722](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/H350722)
- **HQ H350722, foreign platform:** "licensed customs brokers engaged by the Unlicensed Company must carefully structure their transactions in order to abide by 19 C.F.R. § 111.3(a)." — [HQ H350722](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/H350722)
- **§ 111.37 text:** quoted under Q1. HQ H276784 explains the purpose of § 111.37 (citing HRL 225023) as "to prevent a licensed broker from serving as broker in name only, which would occur where actual Customs entry would be prepared by an unlicensed person." — [HQ H276784](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/H276784)
- **§ 111.30(c):** "A broker who changes his or her name, or who proposes to operate under a trade or fictitious name ... must submit to the appropriate Executive Director, Office of Trade ... evidence of his or her authority to use that name. The name must not be used until the approval of Headquarters has been received. In the case of a trade or fictitious name, the broker must affix his own name in conjunction with each signature." — [eCFR 111.30](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-111/subpart-C/section-111.30)
- **Records:**
  - § 111.21(a): the broker keeps "copies of all his correspondence and other records relating to his customs business."
  - § 111.23: originals are kept "within the customs territory of the United States," for 5 years, and POAs until revoked plus 5 years.
  - Sources: [eCFR 111.21](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-111/subpart-C/section-111.21); [eCFR 111.23](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-111/subpart-C/section-111.23)
- **Supervision of unlicensed solicitors:** Without a bona fide employment relationship, "special caution must be taken to ensure adequate supervision of the solicitation activities," and the broker should make sure the unlicensed solicitor's staff are "directly warned not to perform any task ... that may be considered customs business." — [HQ H302355](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/H302355)
- A broker can legitimately hold DOT freight forwarder authority itself as part of its brokerage business. Section 111.36 is then inapplicable because no unlicensed person is involved. — [HQ 116443](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/116443)

### Inferences
- A compliant embedded flow looks like this:
  - The broker's own POA and terms are presented and executed between the importer and the broker, for example by an e-signature flow in the broker's name that lands with the broker.
  - The 111.29(b) notice is on or attached to the POA.
  - The POA or terms include specific written consent under 111.24 for the broker to share records with the platform.
  - The broker is clearly identified as the party doing customs business, and the importer can contact the broker directly.
  - The broker, not platform logic, decides classification, values and refund data elements.
  - The platform gets no fee tied to entries, refunds or claims.
- A pure white-label, where the importer sees only the software company's brand doing "filing," is high-risk under 111.37. It is also inconsistent with the importer knowing who its broker is, which is the policy behind (c)(1) and (c)(3).
- An alternative is for the software company to obtain its own corporate broker license and national permit, with a licensed officer (111.11, 111.19, 111.28). Then 111.36(b) is not triggered (cf. HQ 116443). Not researched in depth.

### Gaps
- No primary-source detail found on how Flexport, Zonos or other digital platforms contract with brokers. Not reached in this pass.
- No ruling found addressing a platform-branded ("powered by Broker X") arrangement under 111.30(c) and 111.37 specifically.

## 5. State-law and other angles (customs attorneys, ABA Model Rules)

### Takeaway
If the software company partners with a customs attorney instead of a broker, lawyer ethics rules apply in addition to Part 111:
- ABA Model Rule 5.4(a) bars sharing legal fees with nonlawyers.
- Model Rule 7.2(b) bars paying for recommendations, but permits paying for advertising and for lead generation that does not "recommend" the lawyer.

State variants control. Part 111 also has a broker-specific attorney rule, 111.35.

### Cited Findings
- **19 CFR 111.35:** "With respect to customs transactions, a broker must not demand or accept from any attorney (whether directly or indirectly, including, for example, from a client as a part of any arrangement with an attorney) on account of any case litigated in any court of law or on account of any other legal service rendered by an attorney any fee or remuneration in excess of an amount measured by or commensurate with the time, effort and skill expended by the broker in performing his services." — [eCFR 111.35](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-111/subpart-C/section-111.35)
- **WSBA Advisory Opinion 201401 (2014):** Paying an online lead-generation service is permissible only if it does not recommend, endorse or vouch for the lawyer, and it matches on disclosed objective criteria (geography, years of practice, practice area), akin to a directory. It is an impermissible paid referral service if it makes subjective matches, analyzes the client's problem, or vouches for the lawyer. Washington comment quoted: "Lawyers are not permitted to pay others for channeling professional work." — [WSBA AO 201401](https://ao.wsba.org/print.aspx?ID=1680)
- **NYSBA Ethics Opinion 1271:** Found a fee-sharing arrangement with a non-lawyer violated Rule 5.4(a), and paying for client referrals violated Rule 7.2(a). Seen via search summary only, not read in full. — [NYSBA Op. 1271](https://nysba.org/ethics-opinion-1271-sharing-of-legal-fees-with-non-lawyer/)
- New York's Rule 7.2 expressly permits paying lead generators that do not recommend the lawyer, provided the payment complies with fee-division and independence rules. This is a secondary summary of NY text and was not verified. — [NYSBA 2017 lead generation opinion summary](https://ezel.ai/ethics-opinions/nysba/1131-paying-a-for-profit-lead-generation-service)
- D.C.'s Rule 5.4 permits fee sharing within organizations with nonlawyer owners, with safeguards. Other states vary. — [DC Bar Rule 5.4](https://www.dcbar.org/for-lawyers/legal-ethics/rules-of-professional-conduct/law-firms-and-associations/professional-independence-of-a-lawyer)

### Inferences
- From background knowledge only; the ABA site blocked automated retrieval, so this is not verified against the primary text:
  - ABA Model Rule 5.4(a) states that a lawyer "shall not share legal fees with a nonlawyer," with narrow exceptions (deceased-lawyer payments, purchase of a practice, nonlawyer employee retirement and profit-sharing plans, and court-awarded fees shared with a nonprofit).
  - Model Rule 7.2(b) bars giving "anything of value to a person for recommending the lawyer's services," with exceptions for reasonable advertising costs, qualified referral services, purchase of a practice, non-exclusive reciprocal referral agreements with disclosure, and nominal gifts.
  - Comment [5] permits paying lead generators that do not recommend the lawyer.
  - The report writer should mark these as unverified quotes or verify them on americanbar.org.
- For a software company working with a customs attorney, a flat advertising or listing fee for a directory-style placement is the generally accepted model. Per-matter or percentage fees risk 5.4(a), and "recommending" the attorney risks 7.2(b). Some states (Arizona, Utah, D.C.) allow nonlawyer ownership or fee sharing under specific regimes. Not researched.

### Gaps
- The ABA Model Rules 5.4 and 7.2 primary text and comments could not be fetched (americanbar.org returned a Cloudflare challenge or 403).
- No ABA formal opinion specifically on paid lead generators was located. ABA Formal Op. 501 (2022, solicitation) surfaced only in secondary results.
- No state-law angle specific to customs brokers was found (brokers are federally licensed). General state consumer-protection and UDAP law on referral-fee disclosure was not researched.
