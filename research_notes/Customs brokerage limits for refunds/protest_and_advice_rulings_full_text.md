# Protests and the advice/analysis line: CBP rulings read in full

Scope: US law as of 2026-10-16. Every ruling below was read in **full text** from the CBP CROSS API (`https://rulings.cbp.gov/api/ruling/{number}`) unless marked otherwise. Regulation text comes from the eCFR versioner API (2026-10-01 point in time). Statute text comes from Cornell LII. The 2022 final rule text comes from the GPO govinfo HTML (FR Doc 2022-22445, 87 FR 63267–63321). Quotes are verbatim. Brackets and ellipses are in the originals unless noted.

Rulings read in full: H272798 (2017), 114199 (1998), 113715 (1997), H350722 (2026), H326926 (2023), H290535 (2022), 115248 (2001), 114404 (1999), 114654 (1999), H175280 (2012), H167815 (2013). Holdings only were read for H097816, H318461 and 113794.

Corrections to the earlier notes:
- H272798's full text cites "HQ 114119 (February 26, 1998)" for the protest exception. The date and quoted language match **HQ 114199**, so "114119" looks like a typo in the ruling.
- H272798 cites the agent-filing provision as "174.12(a)(6)". The current eCFR numbers it **174.12(a)(7)**.
- The 2022 final rule preamble cites "H272798 (January 27, 2018)" and "H115248 (August 28, 2011)". The rulings themselves are dated Jan. 26, 2017 and HQ 115248, Aug. 28, 2001. These are citation errors in the Federal Register text, not different rulings.

## Q1. Can an unlicensed, non-attorney consultant prepare and file a protest for an importer, for pay, under a 174.3 power of attorney?

### Takeaway
Yes, on CBP's own published position. Since 1998 CBP has consistently held, and repeated in 2001, 1999 and 2017, that an unlicensed agent holding a proper power of attorney may **prepare, sign and file** protests. CBP calls this "an exception to the rule that prohibits the involvement of unlicensed consultants in actual transactions with Customs". Its basis is that the protest regulations, and the statute behind them (19 U.S.C. 1514(c)(2)(F), "any authorized agent"), let any authorized agent file. No later ruling or the 2022 final rule limits or reverses this. The earlier researcher who inferred the opposite from 1641(a)(2) was reasoning from the general definition. CBP has not applied that definition to protests. No CBP ruling addresses fees or contingency fees for unlicensed protest agents.

### Cited Findings

**The governing text**
- 19 CFR 174.3(a) (current): "When a protest is filed by a person acting as agent or attorney in fact for the principal, other than an attorney at law or a customhouse broker or his authorized employee acting in his behalf, there shall have been filed or shall be filed with the protest a power of attorney which either specifically authorizes such agent to make, sign, and file the protest or grants unlimited authority to such agent." By contrast, a broker "shall have, however, a general power of attorney to transact Customs business for the principal on Customs Form 5291" (174.3(a)(2)). Last amended by CBP Dec. 16-26, 81 FR 93025 (Dec. 20, 2016). — [eCFR 19 CFR 174.3](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-174/subpart-B/section-174.3)
- 19 CFR 174.12(a)(7): protests may be filed by "Any authorized agent of any of the persons described in paragraphs (a)(1) through (6) of this section, subject to the provisions of § 174.3." — [eCFR 19 CFR 174.12](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-174/subpart-B/section-174.12)
- The statute: 19 U.S.C. 1514(c)(2) lets protests be filed by the importer or consignee, payers of charges, persons seeking entry, drawback claimants, and certain exporters or producers, and "(F) any authorized agent of any of the persons described in clauses (A) through (E)." — [19 U.S.C. 1514 (Cornell LII)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/19/1514)
- The competing text: 19 U.S.C. 1641(a)(2) defines customs business to include transactions concerning "the refund, rebate, or drawback thereof" and "the preparation of documents or forms in any format ... intended to be filed with the Customs Service in furtherance of such activities, whether or not signed or filed by the preparer". The regulatory version in 19 CFR 111.1 adds that customs business "does not include a corporate compliance activity." Corporate compliance activity is limited to related business entities and "does not extend to the actual preparation or filing of the documents." — [eCFR 19 CFR 111.1](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-111/subpart-A/section-111.1)
- 19 CFR 111.2(a)(2) lists the transactions that need no license: own account, broker employee, marine, in-bond, noncommercial, FTZ. **Protests are not listed.** The protest carve-out rests on CBP's rulings and Part 174, not on 111.2. — [eCFR 19 CFR 111.2](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-111/subpart-A/section-111.2)

**HQ 114199 (Feb. 26, 1998), full text.** Addressed to an unlicensed former Customs employee who wanted to work as a drawback consultant. Proposed task 4 was to "Draft, sign and file protests on behalf of clients after obtaining the necessary powers of attorney."
- "Finally, you are correct in asserting that you may prepare, sign and file protests if you have obtained authority from an importer in the form of a properly executed power of attorney. This is an exception to the rule that prohibits the involvement of unlicensed consultants in actual transactions with Customs. The authority for this exception is found in 19 CFR §174.3."
- Holding: the consultant "cannot prepare, or assist a client in preparing, documents and forms intended to be filed with the Customs Service in furtherance of any customs business activity, **unless the document is a protest and the consultant has been issued a power of attorney to file the protest**." (emphasis added)
- Source: [HQ 114199](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/114199)
- Note that 114199 addresses the same Mod Act "whether or not signed or filed by the preparer" language the opposing researcher relied on, and still carves out protests: "you would be conducting customs business without a license if you prepare, or help clients to prepare, forms or documents that will be filed with Customs. The fact that the client will sign and file them is immaterial." — [HQ 114199](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/114199)

**HQ 114404 (Mar. 16, 1999), DuPont, full text.** Its Issue 7 is the "Preparation and filing of protests" by IMEX, an unlicensed DuPont unit, for separate companies.
- "The Customs regulations specifically allow the filing of protests and ruling requests by authorized agents of the importer (see 19 CFR §174.3 and 19 CFR §177.1(c), respectively). The definition of 'agent' in these two circumstances is not limited to customs brokers but may be any agent duly empowered to represent the importer for such purposes. Therefore, IMEX and DIO may perform these functions upon obtaining the proper authorization from the client. See HRL 114199, supra." — [HQ 114404](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/114404)
- The same ruling limits follow-up contact: "it would be improper for an unlicensed consultant to appear alone before Customs to discuss customs business matters on their client's behalf. Only licensed brokers, or in some cases attorneys, may represent a client before Customs on issues involving customs business without the client being present." — [HQ 114404](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/114404)

**HQ 115248 (Aug. 28, 2001), Casio, full text.** CCA is an unlicensed affiliate that "files rulings and protests on their behalf and ... bills the subsidiaries for its services."
- "Under current regulations, any authorized agent of the importer of record may file protests and ruling requests. (19 CFR §§174.3 and 174.12(a)(6); 19 CFR §177.1(c)). Therefore, CCA, if duly authorized as an agent, may file ruling requests and protests on behalf of its sister corporations."
- Holding: "CCA, if duly authorized by the importer, may file ruling requests and protests on behalf of its sister corporations, because current regulations allow any authorized agent to do so."
- CCA billed for its services, and CBP did not treat the billing as defeating the protest carve-out. The protest discussion itself does not mention fees.
- Source: [HQ 115248](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/115248)

**HQ H272798 (Jan. 26, 2017), full text.** An unlicensed Canadian consultant (Company A) was paid by a multinational client and asked about "preparing and filing of protests with CBP".
- "With regard to protests, pursuant to 19 C.F.R. § 174.3, an unlicensed consultant can prepare, sign, and file a protest if a properly executed power of attorney has been obtained." The ruling quotes 174.3(a) and cites "174.12(a)(6) (stating that a protest may be filed by '{a}ny authorized agent')" and "HQ 114119 [sic, 114199] (February 26, 1998) (noting that preparing, signing, and filing protests under proper authority from the importer 'is an exception to the rule that prohibits the involvement of unlicensed consultants in actual transactions with Customs')."
- "Therefore, Company A, if duly authorized as an agent, may file ruling requests and protests on behalf of the client without violating 19 U.S.C. § 1641(b)(1)."
- Holding: "Company A would not be unlawfully engaged in the conduct of 'customs business' by reason of preparing ruling requests and protests, assisting in the preparation of corporate import manuals and desk procedures, conducting post-entry audit reviews (to the extent the reviews will not lead to the preparation of Customs documents or result in activities leading to the filing of such documents), and filing prior disclosures."
- The ruling is limited to its facts: "The holding set forth above applies only to the specific factual situation and procedures and processes identified in the ruling request." Under 19 CFR 177.9(b)(1), a change in facts "may affect the determination".
- Source: [HQ H272798](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/H272798)

**The 2022 broker modernization final rule (87 FR 63267, Oct. 18, 2022), full HTML text searched**
- The word "protest" appears once, in a passage about the transfer of trade functions to the Centers ("decisions and processing related to all types of protests"). The rule does **not** discuss unlicensed protest agents, Part 174, or the 114199/H272798 protest exception. It neither confirms nor limits it. — [87 FR 63267, govinfo HTML](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2022-10-18/html/2022-22445.htm)
- The rule does endorse H272798 as general guidance. CBP declined to redefine customs business and said: "CBP issued a Headquarters Ruling Letter (Headquarters ruling) H272798 (January 26, 2017), which provided an in-depth analysis of what customs business entails in several different scenarios provided by the ruling requester. The ruling serves as guidance to other brokers who encounter the same scenarios. CBP does not believe that further explanations or clarifications are needed." It added that "decisions as to what constitutes customs business are made in a case-by-case analysis". — [87 FR 63273](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2022-10-18/html/2022-22445.htm)
- The rule also amended Part 111 only. The amendment history of 174.3 shows nothing after the 2016 Centers rule. — [eCFR 19 CFR 174.3](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-174/subpart-B/section-174.3)

**Later rulings**
- A CROSS search for "protest unlicensed power of attorney" (sorted newest first) returned only H272798 and 114199 on point, plus unrelated protest decisions. A search for "customs business protest consultant" also found nothing newer that touches the exception. H350722 (2026) and H326926 (2023) do not discuss protests. I found no ruling that limits or revokes the exception. — [CROSS search API](https://rulings.cbp.gov/api/search?term=protest%20unlicensed%20power%20of%20attorney&collection=ALL&commodityGrouping=ALL&pageSize=50&page=1&sortBy=DATE_DESC)

**Fees and contingency fees**
- None of 114199, 114404, 115248 or H272798 says anything about how the protest agent is paid. In 114199 and H272798 the consultant was plainly a paid commercial service, and in 115248 CCA "bills the subsidiaries for its services." CBP approved protest filing in all three. — [HQ 114199](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/114199); [HQ H272798](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/H272798); [HQ 115248](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/115248)
- CROSS searches for "contingency fee" and "contingent fee" returned only valuation and royalty rulings. Nothing addressed consultant or protest fees. — [CROSS search](https://rulings.cbp.gov/api/search?term=contingency%20fee&collection=ALL&commodityGrouping=ALL&pageSize=30&page=1&sortBy=RELEVANCE)
- The fee rules in 19 CFR 111.36(b) bind **brokers**, not unlicensed protest agents dealing directly with the importer. HQ 113715 Scenario 1 found a violation only because the consultant's fee came from a broker performing customs business (drawback) for the consultant's client: "Your company, an unlicensed customs consultant, would be receiving a monetary benefit (i.e., fees paid to you from your client) stemming from a contractual agreement between your company and the broker for the transaction of Customs business by the broker for your client." — [HQ 113715](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/113715)

### Inferences
- CBP's basis is regulatory and statutory authorization rather than a reading of 1641(a)(2). 1514(c)(2)(F) and 174.12(a)(7) name "any authorized agent" as a proper protest filer, and CBP reads "agent" as "not limited to customs brokers" (114404). CBP has never explained how this squares with 1641(a)(2)'s "refund" and "preparation ... whether or not signed or filed by the preparer" language. It simply calls protests an "exception" (114199). The cleanest reconciliation is the specific statute (1514) controlling the general one (1641), but that is my inference. CBP has not written it.
- On the text, the exception covers **preparing, signing and filing** the protest (114199 and H272798 both say "prepare, sign, and file"), not only filing.
- Because no ruling or regulation conditions the exception on the fee, a paid protest service is consistent with the rulings. A contingency fee charged directly to the importer is not addressed. Nothing found prohibits it under customs law, but that comes from silence, not a holding.
- The exception has edges that the rulings flag:
  - (i) The unlicensed agent may not appear alone before CBP to discuss the matter (114404, 114654).
  - (ii) The audit or analysis that *leads to* the protest is judged separately (see Q2).
  - (iii) A protest is a different instrument from a PSC or post-entry amendment, which H272798 says are customs business.
  - (iv) If a broker files the protest and shares the fee with the software company, 111.36(b) and 113715 apply.
- The CAPE/IEEPA refund channel and PSCs are not protests. The protest exception should not be stretched to them (see the drawback_psc_cape_filing.md note).

### Gaps
- **Unauthorized practice of law.** CBP rulings address only 1641 and Part 111. Whether drafting a protest's legal arguments for pay is the practice of law under state law, or under any federal "practice before the agency" rule, is outside CBP's rulings. I found no source on it. It should be checked separately.
- No court decision was found testing the 114199/H272798 protest exception against 1641(a)(2). It remains CBP's administrative position, which is binding only as to the ruling recipients under 177.9. Treat it as CBP's stated guidance, not settled law.
- Contingency fees for protest agents: no primary authority found either way.
- Whether the ACE Protest module lets a non-broker, non-importer agent file electronically was not checked. Paper filing on CBP Form 19 is available under 174.12(b).

## Q2. Where is the line on advice and analysis, and which of HTS Hero's features (a)–(d) are customs business?

### Takeaway
CBP's line is between **general information** (allowed) and **entry-specific determinations that will or may end up on a filing** (customs business). Post-entry review that only *identifies discrepancies* in past entries is allowed (H175280, H272798). Deciding the corrected value, classification or quantity, "resolving discrepancies", or verifying duties where the corrected figures "will ultimately appear on the entry" is customs business (H272798, 115248, H167815). 114199's broader 1998 ban on advice about particular entries "filed or to be filed" has been narrowed in practice by the later audit rulings, but CBP has never withdrawn it. Applying this: (a) flagging is closest to the permitted H175280 audit, (c) is closest to general HTS information, (b) depends on whether it determines eligibility for a specific entry, and (d) carries the most risk if it tells the broker what to file.

### Cited Findings

**General advice vs. particular transactions**
- HQ 114199: "a license is not required to give instruction and general advice on various Customs topics ... We permit these types of educational activities because they have no direct relationship to actual transactions with Customs." But: "We would, however, draw the line at unlicensed persons advising clients on issues involving particular entries or drawback claims **filed or to be filed** with Customs. This is because 19 U.S.C. §1641(a)(2) specifically states that transactions involving the entry and admissibility of merchandise, the payment of duties thereon, and the refund or drawback of duties, fall within the definition of customs business. For this reason, you may not advise brokers or other clients on actual drawback contracts or claims. Furthermore, you would be precluded from entering into discussions between Customs and a client on issues pertaining to such transactions." Holding: the consultant "may not offer advice, or participate in discussions with Customs, on issues relating to a particular transaction with Customs." (emphasis added) — [HQ 114199](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/114199)
- HQ 114404 restates the same line and applies it beyond drawback: "the same principles apply here. It is Customs position that the giving of general advice on how to classify, value or mark merchandise does not fall under the definition of 'customs business.' For example, an unlicensed person could explain to a third party the use of the Harmonized Tariff Schedules, the General Rules of Interpretation, and the Explanatory Notes, or provide an overview of the different methods of valuation. That same person would be precluded, however, from classifying a client's merchandise that is going to be the subject of an entry filed with Customs." — [HQ 114404](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/114404)
- HQ 114654 (May 28, 1999): unlicensed persons "may not, however, advise a client on how to classify, appraise, or mark merchandise that is going to be the subject of an entry," and may advise "provided such advice is general and does not include specifics on how to classify, value, or mark an item which is going to be the subject of a particular import entry." — [HQ 114654](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/114654)

**Post-entry audits and duty verification (most relevant to feature (a))**
- HQ H175280 (Sept. 24, 2012): a software-based post-entry audit compared "electronic documentation submitted via K&A's software against the original source entry documents filed with CBP to determine if there are discrepancies with regard to value, classification, and quantity entered." Discrepancies went "to K&A's brokerage office for review and resolution by a licensed customs broker," and the auditors "will not interact with clients or CBP officials, nor interpret or resolve any discrepancies". CBP held: "Unlicensed entities may give general advice on customs-related issues, and conduct post-entry audits provided the services rendered do not amount to the transaction of customs business. Since the employees ... will not prepare documents for filing with CBP or engage in activities leading to the filing of such documents, the identification of discrepancies between entry documents filed with CBP and clients' records is not customs business." — [HQ H175280](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/H175280)
- HQ H272798 (2017) on audits "designed to ensure that the clients have paid the correct amount of duties ... and to identify duty recovery opportunities," using software to "review entry summaries, liquidation records, and duty payments" after liquidation:
  - "To the extent that Company A's post-entry audits and compliance reviews will not lead to the preparation of Customs documents or engage in activities leading to the filing of such documents, the reviews are not 'customs business' and can be carried out by an unlicensed entity. To be clear, Post-Entry Amendments and Post-Summary Corrections are considered 'customs business' and must be performed by a licensed customs broker."
  - "If, however, 'identifying duty recovery opportunities' requires Company A to make decisions with regard to value, classification, and quantity entered, or resolving discrepancies or irregularities in CBP transactions, such activities constitute 'customs business' and would not be permissible activities for an unlicensed entity."
  - CBP found the description "sufficiently vague that a definitive ruling is not possible."
  - Source: [HQ H272798](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/H272798)
- HQ 115248 (2001): "The verification of tariff numbers prior to entry is a customs business activity when the possibility exists that corrected classification information derived from the verification process will end up on the entry." On duty checking, a review "checking whether the duty rates applied to the merchandise properly attach to the selected tariff numbers, and then determining whether the projected duties were correctly calculated ... may only be performed by the importer or by a licensed broker when the possibility exists that corrected duty information derived from the duty verification process will ultimately appear on the entry." — [HQ 115248](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/115248)
- HQ H167815 (June 28, 2013), DHL: "such verification activity that could result in a correction submitted to CBP must be conducted by a licensed customs broker." Reviewing full entry and entry summary data to resolve billing disputes was held to be customs business. Reviewing only the air waybill and bill was not. — [HQ H167815](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/H167815)

**Classification, databases and tools (most relevant to (b), (c) and (d))**
- H272798 database holding: a classification database used as a "resource" with a "meaningfully implemented" disclaimer "does not cross the line." The distinction is that it "will not 'direct' the client or the customs broker in the preparation of the entry." CBP called it a "fine line." It also cites HQ H068278 (2009): software that "determined classification and other entry-related information" was customs business "even though the entry forms were ultimately filed by a U.S. customs broker." — [HQ H272798](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/H272798)
- HQ H290535 (Sept. 29, 2022), Hampton: providing 8- and 10-digit subheadings for specific goods customers will import, with an "advisory only" disclaimer, "violates 19 U.S.C. § 1641(b)(1) by conducting customs business without a license." CBP explained that "because Hampton's customers request the subheadings before importing the goods ... there is a strong possibility that the customers will use subheadings provided on the entry filed with CBP." Six-digit classification "does not constitute customs business because the six-digit HTS provision is insufficient for entry." — [HQ H290535](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/H290535)
- HQ H350722 (Jan. 16, 2026), full text, on an unlicensed online platform:
  - Nexus test: "CBP has consistently looked to whether the classification is tied to a prospective entry. A nexus to a prospective entry can be established by the number of digits provided and the context in which the classification information is provided."
  - Ten-digit rule: "if the Unlicensed Company's AI classification tool is deriving subheadings to the ten-digit level for merchandise that has been imported, or is intended to be imported, then customs business is being impermissibly conducted". Note the phrase "has been imported".
  - Permitted lane: "to the extent that the tool is providing customers with general classification information that is disconnected from an actual or intended importation of merchandise requiring entry, then so long as the Unlicensed Company's disclaimer is meaningfully implemented, we find that this tool is akin to the narrowly permissible database in HQ H272798."
  - Data selection: "whether data is extracted manually or automatically though an OCR tool, CBP has repeatedly held that an unlicensed entity cannot decide what data should appear on an entry." Also: "the filing of an entry is not a prerequisite to such an activity constituting customs business."
  - Tools: "A tool does not constitute a 'person' ... such that the actual decision regarding the classification of imported merchandise, or any other information needed to make entry, must be made by a duly licensed customs broker."
  - Importer self-upload: "if an importer is disclosing entry data and documentation to the Unlicensed Company by uploading such information to the online platform ... no violation of 19 C.F.R. § 111.24 occurs".
  - Source: [HQ H350722](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/H350722)
- HQ H326926 (Dec. 19, 2023): "Identifying entry-related data and keying it into an ABI system falls squarely within the scope of preparing parts of an entry intended to be filed with CBP," even though the broker "would audit the data, make necessary amendments, and transmit". — [HQ H326926](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/H326926)
- 2022 final rule preamble: "generally, classification determinations at the eight- and ten-digit HTSUS levels are considered customs business," and classification at six or four digits is not. — [87 FR 63274](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2022-10-18/html/2022-22445.htm)

**Prior disclosures (relevant if an audit finds underpayment)**
- H272798: "there is nothing that precludes Company A from filing a prior disclosure on behalf of a client if given proper authorization," and "prior disclosures relate to transactions that are already completed and do not constitute 'customs business'". — [HQ H272798](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/H272798)

**Trade programs and origin (feature (b))**
- No ruling read addresses a tool that identifies which preference programs or exemptions a product *might* qualify for. 114404 notes that unlicensed IMEX "monitors the duty preference programs in which DuPont is engaged" without ruling on that activity. It also holds that preparing NAFTA certificates of origin for US *exports* is not customs business because the certificate is not "intended to be filed with the U.S. Customs Service". — [HQ 114404](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/114404)

### Inferences
These apply the quoted text to HTS Hero. They are not CBP holdings.

- **(a) Comparing the duty paid on a past entry with the duty in force on its entry date, and flagging over- or underpayment.**
  - Closest authorities: H175280 (software "identification of discrepancies" is not customs business) and H272798 (post-liquidation audits are allowed unless they lead to preparing documents).
  - Risk factors from the text: the tool "make[s] decisions with regard to value, classification, and quantity", "resolv[es] discrepancies", or produces "corrected duty information" that "will ultimately appear" on a PSC or other filing (115248, H167815, H272798).
  - The tool takes the HTS code as entered and applies the rate in force, which sits near the "identify the discrepancy" end. It becomes customs business if it proposes a *different* classification for the past entry, or outputs the corrected figures for a PSC. H350722's "has been imported" phrasing suggests 10-digit reclassification of past entries is covered too.
  - 114199's 1998 language ("particular entries ... filed or to be filed") is in tension with H175280 and H272798. CBP has not reconciled them explicitly. The later rulings are the more specific guidance on audits.
  - If the result feeds a protest that the founder prepares under a 174.3 POA, the protest exception (Q1) arguably covers the document. H272798 does not say whether the audit leading to a protest is covered. **Unsettled.**
- **(b) Identifying which trade programs or exemptions a product might qualify for.** No direct ruling. By analogy to the general-vs-specific line (114404, 114654), general eligibility information ("goods of HTS X from country Y may be eligible for program Z if conditions are met") looks like general advice. Deciding that a specific entry qualifies, and supplying the SPI or claim data to file, looks like "deciding what data should appear on an entry" (H350722) or a refund determination on a particular transaction (114199). **Unsettled.**
- **(c) Showing rates for an HTS code plus origin supplied by the user.** This does not classify merchandise. It reports published tariff law for a code the user chose, which is closest to "explain the use of the Harmonized Tariff Schedules" (114404). No ruling treats rate lookup as customs business. The risk reappears only if the tool picks or suggests the 8- or 10-digit code for goods that are or will be imported (H290535, H350722).
- **(d) A report the importer hands to its broker.**
  - Permitted model (H272798): the report is a "resource" with a meaningfully implemented disclaimer that leaves the broker to make its own decision.
  - Prohibited model (H068278 as described in H272798, H290535, H350722): the report "direct[s]" the broker, for example by supplying specific 10-digit codes for goods being entered, or the corrected values for a PSC, so that the broker does nothing "other than the act of filing itself."
  - H290535 shows a disclaimer does not cure entry-specific subheadings. A report on *past* entries that lists discrepancies for the broker to evaluate matches the H175280 facts, where discrepancies went to licensed brokers who "determine if the errors were actually made". That is the best-supported structure.
- **Fees.** A fee the importer pays the software company is not customs business by itself. It becomes a 111.36(b) problem for the broker only if it is tied to customs business the broker performs (H350722, 113715).

### Gaps
- CBP has not ruled on a self-serve SaaS tool that compares duty paid with duty owed on past entries. H175280 (an audit firm with a brokerage arm) and H272798 (found "too vague" on audits) are the closest. A 19 CFR 177 ruling request is the only way to get certainty.
- No ruling addresses trade-program eligibility screening (feature (b)).
- The tension between 114199 ("filed or to be filed") and the later audit rulings has never been addressed by CBP. Both remain published, and neither has been revoked.
- Holdings for H097816, H318461 and 113794 were read only in part. They concern referrals, forwarder duty remittance and broker refund checks, and are not central here.
